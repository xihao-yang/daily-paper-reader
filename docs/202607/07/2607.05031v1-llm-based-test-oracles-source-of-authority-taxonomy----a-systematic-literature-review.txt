# LLM-Based Test Oracles: Source-of-Authority Taxonomy—A Systematic Literature Review 

Ali Hassaan Mughal iD , Member, IEEE, and Muhammad Bilal iD 

Abstract —Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to produce test oracles, the part of a test that decides whether observed behavior is correct. Yet a clear account of where these oracles draw their authority is missing. Prior secondary studies organize the area by oracle form or by LLM technique. None organizes it by the source of the verdict’s authority, the property that governs how far a verdict can be trusted. This article presents a systematic literature review, conducted and reported under the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. From 2,436 records, an LLM pre-filter followed by independent dual human screening (reviewer agreement, a Cohen’s kappa of 0.79) and full-text assessment yielded 54 included studies. We analyze these along three axes: the source of an oracle’s authority, the form it takes, and the mechanism that adjudicates it. We characterize the landscape of domains, languages, models, and adaptation strategies. Specification-derived authority, though the most common single source, covers about half of the studies (28 of 54). The remaining 26 reach a verdict with no specification at all. The source of authority and the adjudication mechanism cross-cut: the same source is checked by several mechanisms and one mechanism serves several sources, so a label such as LLM-as-a-judge names a mechanism rather than a basis for trust. We further report how these oracles are evaluated and how they fail, and read the sparse and empty regions of the taxonomy as a research agenda. The protocol, search query, and per-study coding sheet are released as supplementary material. 

Index Terms —Large language models, LLM-as-a-judge, oracle problem, software quality assurance, software testing, systematic literature review, test automation, test oracle. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# SOFTWARE now runs in almost every part of daily life, and how much we can trust it depends largely on how well we test it. A test feeds an input to a program, runs it, and then asks one question: is the result correct? The part of the test that answers that question is the test oracle . A test is only as good as its oracle. Deciding what counts as correct is hard. For many programs there is no easy way to know the right answer in advance. This difficulty has a name, the oracle problem . Barr et al. [1] surveyed it before large language models (LLMs) were in wide use. They organized the known ways to obtain an oracle and showed how often a strong oracle is simply missing.

LLMs have changed the picture. An LLM can now write an oracle for a program [2]–[4], or act as the oracle itself by judging the output directly [5]–[7]. This is a fast-growing area [8], [9]: most of the reviewed studies appeared in 2025 or later. The literature describes these new oracles. It characterizes them, however, by a feature that does not, by itself, capture how far to trust the verdict. Papers sort oracles by their shape, such as an assertion [3] or a metamorphic relation [10], or by the technique used, such as “LLM as a judge” [5], [7]. They rarely ask where the verdict’s authority comes from, yet that is what sets the ceiling on how much to trust it. Two oracles can look identical yet rest on different ground. One assertion may encode a written specification; another, with the same syntax, may encode only what the model guessed. This review organizes the literature by that missing feature: the source of authority , where the correctness of the verdict ultimately comes from. We make three contributions. First, we give a taxonomy of oracle sources that extends Barr et al. into the LLM era. We keep it separate from two questions the literature mixes in: the form of the oracle and the mechanism 

that computes pass or fail. Second, we apply this lens in a systematic literature review, conducted and reported under the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [11], that screens 2,436 records down to 54 included studies. Third, we report what the lens reveals: specification-independence follows almost entirely from the source; source and mechanism cross-cut rather than coincide; two source categories go unoccupied by LLM-supplied oracles; and the most frequently reported risks are weak oracles and model hallucination. A. What a test oracle is 

Every test has four parts. There is an input ; the program under test , often called the system under test; the output 

the program produces; and the oracle , the part that decides whether the output is correct (Fig. 1). The first three parts are usually easy to set up. The oracle is the difficult one. B. Why it is hard 

That “from somewhere” is the core difficulty. For 

isLeapYear the right answers are known, so an oracle is easy. For most real programs they are not known in advance and are costly to work out by hand. This is the oracle problem :the difficulty of obtaining a reliable way to judge whether an output is correct. Over the years, testers have found partial answers that avoid needing the exact right answer for every input. An 

assertion [12] states a condition the output must meet, such as that a year reported as a leap year is divisible by 4. A 

metamorphic relation [13] compares two related runs instead of checking one output against a known value. For example, running the function twice on the same year should give the same result. Differential testing [14] runs two programs that should agree and treats any disagreement as a possible bug. Each of these gives a verdict without a full description of correct behavior. Barr et al. [1] surveyed these and other techniques in detail. 

C. What LLMs add 

An LLM can take part in the oracle in two ways. It can generate an oracle, for example by reading a function’s documentation and writing the assertions that check it [4], [15], [16]. Or it can be the oracle, by looking at an output and judging it directly, the way a human reviewer would [5], [17], [18]. Both were impractical at scale before LLMs, and both are now common. This raises a new question, the subject of this review: when an LLM writes the oracle, or makes the verdict itself, where does the authority for that verdict come from? III. T HE SOURCE -OF -A UTHORITY FRAMEWORK 

The central idea of our framework is to separate three questions that the literature usually treats as one. 

A. Three distinct questions 

When an LLM-based oracle gives a verdict, we can ask three things about it. 1) Source of authority. Where does the correctness of the verdict ultimately come from? 2) Form. What shape does the oracle take, for example an assertion or a metamorphic relation? PREPRINT. SUBMITTED TO IEEE ACCESS FOR POSSIBLE PUBLICATION. 3

3) Mechanism. How is the pass-or-fail decision computed, for example by running an assertion or by asking the model to judge? Most prior work answers only the second or third question. The first sets the ceiling on trust: an oracle is only as trustworthy as the source its authority rests on. 

B. Seven sources of authority 

The framework distinguishes seven sources of authority (Fig. 1). The same program output can be judged by any of them, each drawing on a different authority. In words: an implementation-derived oracle takes the code, or the tests already written for it, as the authority. It accepts what the code currently does, so it catches later changes but not a bug that is already present. A specification-derived 

oracle takes an external description of intended behavior as the authority. The description can be formal, such as a logical postcondition; semi-formal, such as a structured rule or a Gherkin scenario; or natural-language, such as a docstring or a requirement. A reference-differential oracle takes a separate implementation as the authority and flags disagreement. A recent variant uses several implementations the LLM writes itself and treats their agreement as the expected answer [19], [20]. The remaining four are a model-parametric oracle (the model’s own knowledge, with no external reference), a human-elicited oracle (criteria a person supplies), a regression-from-prior-version oracle (an earlier version as the reference), and an implicit-intrinsic oracle (crashes or well-formedness), each defined in Fig. 1. 

C. Specification dependence 

The seven sources split by one question: does the oracle need an external specification? In the idealized split, only specification-derived oracles do; the other six are defined to reach a verdict without one. Section V examines how closely the corpus follows this split, including the few boundary cases that cross it. 

D. Source is not mechanism 

Source and mechanism cross-cut: a single source can be checked by many mechanisms, and a single mechanism can serve many sources. A specification-derived oracle, for ex-ample, can be checked by running an assertion, by matching against an expected value, or by asking the model to judge. “LLM as a judge” is a mechanism, not a source: the judge may be leaning on a specification, on the model’s own knowledge, or on criteria a person gave it. A few mechanisms recur across different sources (Fig. 9). IV. R EVIEW METHOD 

This systematic literature review was planned, conducted, and reported under the PRISMA 2020 statement [11]. The protocol, the frozen search query, the operational criteria, and the per-record screening log are released as supplementary material. 

A. Objective 

The review maps and analyzes the literature on LLM-based test oracles according to the source from which each oracle draws its verdict authority. A study is within scope if it (i) produces a test oracle, or (ii) empirically studies how the oracle is determined, within a pipeline that uses an LLM. Pre-2021 foundational work is retained as background through citation only and is excluded from the primary mapping counts. 

B. Research questions 

The review is organized around eight research questions (RQ1–RQ8), two of which (RQ2 and RQ5) are the main focus. 

RQ1 (Landscape): What software domains, systems under test, languages, models, and adaptation strategies characterize the LLM-based oracle literature? 

RQ2 (Source of authority): From what source does each oracle draw the authority for its verdict? 

RQ3 (Specification dependence): To what extent does the oracle require an external specification, as opposed to deriving expected behavior from the implementation, a reference, or the model itself? 

RQ4 (Form): In what form is the oracle expressed (asser-tion, expected output value, metamorphic relation, property or invariant, classifier label, or exception oracle)? 

RQ5 (Adjudication mechanism and LLM role): How is the pass/fail computed, and what role does the LLM play in that computation? 

RQ6 (Evaluation): How is each oracle evaluated, and how is the oracle’s own correctness assessed? 

RQ7 (Trust and failure modes): What failure modes are reported, and how are they mitigated? 

RQ8 (Gaps): Which regions of the source-by-mechanism space are sparse or empty? A guiding hypothesis, tested in the synthesis, is that the source of authority (RQ2) is independent of the adjudication mechanism (RQ5). The same authority can be checked by different mechanisms, and a single mechanism such as LLM-as-judge can draw on different authorities. 

C. Protocol and registration 

The search query was frozen on 31 May 2026, and the inclusion and exclusion criteria were fixed in writing before screening began. In place of registration in a public registry, this pre-specified protocol is archived openly in the replication package [21]. 

D. Eligibility criteria 

A record was included only if all of the following held: 

I1 (Oracle focus): The work concerns a test oracle, that is, the mechanism that decides expected behavior or pass/fail (asser-tions, expected outputs, properties or invariants, metamorphic relations, differential comparison, or human judgment), rather than only test-input generation or coverage. PREPRINT. SUBMITTED TO IEEE ACCESS FOR POSSIBLE PUBLICATION. 4

I2 (LLM involvement): An LLM generates, infers, or de-termines the oracle, or the study empirically examines how the oracle is determined or behaves in an LLM-based testing pipeline. 

I3 (Window): Published between January 2021 and May 2026. 

I4 (Form): At least four pages, written in English, with retrievable full text, peer reviewed and indexed (conference, journal, or early access). A record was excluded if any of the following held: 

E1: Testing of LLMs or natural language processing (NLP) systems (the LLM is the system under test, for example bias, hallucination, jailbreak, or robustness testing), rather than LLMs used for testing software. 

E2: LLM test or code generation that does not foreground an oracle (input-only, coverage-only, prefix-only, or program-repair-only). 

E3: Hardware-verification assertion or oracle generation, for example in SystemVerilog, register-transfer level (RTL), or very large-scale integration (VLSI). This is noted as an ad-jacent line of work in Section VIII. 

E4: Secondary studies (surveys, systematic reviews, mapping studies). These are excluded from the primary set to avoid double counting, and are retained separately for the discussion of related reviews. 

E5: Non-peer-reviewed preprints. These are deliberately ex-cluded to keep peer review as an evidence-quality bar (crite-rion I4). The resulting under-coverage of preprint venues is reported as a threat to validity (Section VIII). 

E6: Editorials, keynotes, posters, tool demonstrations, tutori-als, extended abstracts, items under four pages, non-English items, and non-retrievable items. 

E. Information sources 

We searched three bibliographic databases under institu-tional subscription: Scopus, IEEE Xplore, and the ACM Digital Library. All searches were executed on 31 May 2026 over the field set title, abstract, and keywords, restricted to the publication window 2021–2026. Scopus indexes a large fraction of IEEE and ACM content and therefore also serves as a cross-database consolidation source.

G. Selection process 

Records exported from the three databases were merged and de-duplicated by normalized DOI, falling back to anormalized title when a DOI was absent. This left 2,245 unique records. Because the high-recall query admitted mostly off-topic records, the corpus was too large to screen every record twice by hand. We therefore screened in two stages, an arrangement disclosed in accordance with PRISMA 2020 item 8 on the use of automation tools. The first stage was a recall-oriented automated pre-filter that removes clearly out-of-scope records; the second was independent human dual screening that verifies the retained set in full and validates the pre-filter’s exclusions on a stratified sample. 

a) Stage 1: automated pre-filter: An LLM (Claude Opus 4.8) [22] classified every one of the 2,245 unique records against the eligibility criteria from title and abstract. It assigned each an include, maybe, or exclude decision and, where applicable, an exclusion reason code (E1–E6). Am-biguous records were assigned maybe and retained, resolving uncertainty toward full-text assessment. This stage retained 178 records and excluded 2,067. 

b) Stage 2: independent human dual screening with val-idation: Two authors then screened a 385-record verification set, independently and blind both to the model’s labels and to each other. The set comprised all 178 model-retained records plus a fixed-seed, reason-stratified 10% random sample of 207 records from the 2,067 model-excluded records. Screening all retained records catches every false positive; the exclusion sample estimates how often the model wrongly discards rel-evant work. Each reviewer recorded an Include, Maybe, or Exclude decision with a one-line rationale for every record. Inter-rater agreement was substantial, measured by Cohen’s 

κ, a chance-corrected measure of how often two review-ers agree. On the operative retain-versus-exclude decision (Retain = Include ∪ Maybe), κ = 0.79 (95% bootstrap confidence interval (CI) [0 .72 , 0.85] ; 90.4% raw agreement). On the finer three-level scale it was κ = 0.64 (95% CI 

[0 .57 , 0.71] ; 81.6% agreement). The 71 disagreements were reconciled conservatively. Include-versus-Maybe splits, where both reviewers leaned toward retention, were retained. The eight direct Include-versus-Exclude conflicts were decided PREPRINT. SUBMITTED TO IEEE ACCESS FOR POSSIBLE PUBLICATION. 5

individually against the full text. Exclude-versus-Maybe splits, where neither reviewer favored inclusion, were excluded. The retain-on-uncertainty principle stated above governs the recall-oriented pre-filter; this human stage is where precision is applied. Reconciliation yielded a consensus of 115 retained records (87 include, 28 maybe) and 270 excluded over the verification set. 

c) Validation of the pre-filter: Treating the human con-sensus as the reference, the model achieved 99.1% recall on the verification set, agreeing with all but one consensus-retained record. That single miss was itself a borderline case reconciled to include only after full-text discussion. On the stratified sample of its own exclusions, the model’s observed false-omission rate was 1/207 = 0 .48% . If this rate held across the 1,860 model-excluded records not individually re-screened, it would imply roughly nine missed relevant records. Because the CI around a single observed omission is wide, this residual risk is carried as a threat to validity (Section VIII). The sampled exclusions and their decisions are also released for audit. The model was otherwise over-inclusive, retaining 64 consensus-excluded records. Its precision is supplied by the human stage. 

H. Data extraction and coding 

Each included study is coded against a 35-field codebook, fixed before extraction and released as supplementary material. The controlled vocabularies are likewise fixed in advance. The codebook is built on the three axes of Section III: the source of authority (RQ2), the form (RQ4), and the adjudication mechanism (RQ5). It also adds descriptive fields for the landscape, the evaluation, and the reported failure modes (RQ1, RQ6, RQ7). Source and mechanism are coded independently (Section III). A primary source and, where a system is compositional, a secondary source are recorded. The synthesis is descriptive: it reports distributions across the taxonomy and reads gaps off the sparse or empty regions of the source-by-mechanism space. Effectiveness numbers are not pooled, because the studies differ too much in benchmark and metric: 59% use a custom benchmark. A lightweight, non-exclusionary quality appraisal of the included studies, scored on eight items derived from these coded fields, is reported in Section V-G.

F. How the oracles fail (RQ7) 

Reported failure modes converge on a small set (Fig. 10). Hallucination is the model asserting behavior the system does not exhibit. It is the hazard studies engage with most, surfacing in 46 of 54 studies. A closer reading separates three overlapping ways studies engage with it (Table IV): 44 name it as a risk, 31 report observing it empirically in their own results, and 35 apply a concrete mitigation. Among the other modes, the leaders are prompt sensitivity (24) and weak or missing assertions (24), where the generated oracle is too loose to detect the faults it should; then flakiness or nondeterminism (11), cost or latency (11), and data contamination (7). Over-assertion, an oracle so strict that it rejects correct behavior, appears in only 4 studies, against 24 for weak or missing assertions, making weak oracles the most frequently reported correctness risk. But how often a failure is reported may not match how often it happens. The 35 mitigations are led by execution-based filtering, compiling and running a candidate oracle and discarding what fails to compile or rejects known-correct behavior [16], [53]; self-consistency or multi-sample voting [19], [20]; and human review (7 studies, Table III).

VI. D ISCUSSION 

A. What the findings mean 

Three results stand out, together showing a field that has changed what an oracle rests on. First, the authority behind LLM-based oracles often does not come from a specification at all. That makes them useful on everyday software, which rarely has a written specification. It also makes them risky: an oracle resting only on the model’s own knowledge, with no external anchor at all, can be wrong with no signal that it is. Second, the source of authority and the way pass or fail is computed are separate choices (Fig. 9). A label such as “LLM-as-judge” therefore says nothing about how far to trust the verdict. Third, the most frequently reported risks concern oracle strength: the most common reported failure is an oracle too weak to catch the bug it should. This is compounded by the model’s tendency to hallucinate behavior the system never shows. 

B. Choosing a source of authority in practice 

The taxonomy also reads as a practical guide: the right source depends on what the project already has. If the code has documentation, such as docstrings or require-ments, specification-derived oracles are the best-supported path. The reviewed work most often turns natural-language documentation into assertions that run against the program [2], [4], [16]. If there is no specification but a trusted second implementa-tion exists, a reference-differential oracle is natural: run both and compare. A newer variant has the LLM write several implementations and treats their agreement as the expected answer, removing the need for an existing reference [19], [20]. If the program is interactive or visual, such as a mobile app or a graphical interface, the field uses model-parametric ora-cles. The LLM judges the output directly from its own knowl-edge, because no executable specification is available [5], [6]. This is the least anchored option, so it pairs best with human review or independent cross-checks. If existing tests already encode expected behavior, implementation-derived oracles can extend them. They inherit the limits of those tests, however, and cannot catch a bug already in the current code. In every case the source should be stated plainly. How the model obtains that authority does not change which source it is. A study that uses retrieval-augmented generation or an agentic tool call to fetch a specification is still specification-derived; one that fetches or executes existing code is still implementation-derived. Fifteen of the 54 studies use RAG or agentic (multi-agent) adaptation (10 and 7 respectively, with 2 studies using both). They span four sources (9 specification-derived, 3 implementation-derived, 2 model-parametric, 1 reference-differential), confirming that adaptation strategy and source of authority are independent choices. 

C. Gaps and a research agenda (RQ8) 

Reading the sparse and empty regions of the source-by-mechanism space (Fig. 9) and the source totals (Fig. 7) yields a concrete agenda. 

Empty source categories. Two of the seven sources are unoccupied. No included study has an LLM generate or determine a regression-from-prior-version oracle: when that prior version is the reference, the comparison is supplied by non-LLM tooling carried over from earlier work. Such studies therefore fall to E2. Likewise no study rests on a purely implicit-intrinsic oracle (crashes or well-formedness) as the LLM’s contribution. Here too the failure signal is supplied without an LLM, so such designs also fall to E2. These two cells are therefore empty partly by construction of our scope. Both do surface as secondary , compositional signals rather than the primary authority, implicit-intrinsic in three studies and regression-from-prior-version in four. Neither ever appears, however, as the oracle an LLM itself supplies or determines. The open question is whether an LLM can add value where it is currently bypassed, which this corpus cannot settle. 

Empty source-by-mechanism cells. LLM-as-judge is never applied to implementation-derived or reference-differential au-thority: when the authority is the code or an independent reference, the included studies always execute or match rather than asking the model to judge. Whether an LLM judge briefed with the implementation or the reference could outperform exact matching, particularly for outputs that are semantically equivalent but textually different, is untested. Human-elicited authority is the sparsest occupied source (2 studies) and appears only with LLM-as-judge or statistical mechanisms. 

Evaluation gaps. The reliance on custom benchmarks (59%) and the thin use of mutation analysis to validate oracle fault detection (6 of 54) together mean the field lacks a shared, fault-grounded yardstick for oracle quality. A standard oracle-evaluation benchmark scored by mutation killing, rather than by textual similarity to a reference oracle, would be valuable. 

Trust gaps. Hallucination is the hazard studies engage with most (46 of 54), but it is named far more often than measured: 44 studies flag it as a risk while only 31 report observing it. The 35 mitigations, in turn, are varied and rarely evaluated for residual risk. The weak-assertion failure mode (24) shows that generated oracles frequently under-constrain behavior in a way that similarity metrics do not detect. Measuring oracle strength , beyond oracle-generation accuracy, is the open problem these findings point to. VII. R ELATED REVIEWS

VIII. T HREATS TO VALIDITY 

A. Search precision and recall 

The recall-tuned query admitted many off-topic records. These were removed by criterion-based screening (Table I). 

B. Automation-assisted screening 

The pre-filter’s exclusions carry the only residual risk: the model-excluded records not individually re-screened. The audited sample puts the false-omission rate at 0.48% (1/207), projecting to roughly nine missed records across the 1,860 unaudited exclusions. Because the CI around a single observed omission is wide, this residual risk is disclosed here, with the sampled decisions released for audit. It is unlikely to change the main taxonomy-level findings. 

C. Database scope 

The search covered Scopus, IEEE Xplore, and the ACM Digital Library; Web of Science, Springer Link, and dedicated preprint servers were not queried, and preprint-only work is therefore under-represented (criterion E5). 

D. Hardware-verification boundary 

LLM-based assertion generation for hardware (criterion E3) is a sizable adjacent literature that we deliberately place out of scope. It is a natural target for a companion review. 

E. Extraction and classification 

The source-of-authority code is the review’s central inter-pretive judgment and is, like any qualitative coding, subject to reasonable disagreement at category boundaries. To make it auditable, every included and excluded record is released with its eligibility decision, the criterion applied, the primary and secondary source codes, and an anchoring quotation. The load-bearing boundary rules, notably runtime execution versus static proof and the LLM as oracle versus as test-input generator, are stated explicitly so that any classification can be re-traced and contested. IX. C ONCLUSION 

Test oracles decide whether software behaves correctly, and LLMs have made it possible to generate them or to use a model as one. This review organized that new body of work by a question the literature usually skips: where does the verdict’s authority come from? Across 54 studies, most oracles take their authority from a written description, but just under half need no specification at all. The source almost entirely determines which is which. The source of authority cross-cuts how pass or fail is computed, so describing an oracle only by its mechanism leaves out what matters most. The most frequently reported weaknesses are oracles too weak to catch bugs, and verdicts the model can hallucinate. Its evaluation usually checks resemblance to a known oracle, and rarely whether the oracle catches faults. The source-of-authority taxonomy reads this literature on the grounds that set how far a verdict can be trusted. For practitioners, the first question about any LLM oracle should be where its authority comes from, since that predicts where it will fail more reliably than the mechanism does.
