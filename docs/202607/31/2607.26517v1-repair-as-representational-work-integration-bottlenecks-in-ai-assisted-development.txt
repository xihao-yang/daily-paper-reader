# Abstract 

Research on AI -assisted programming has concentrated on the gulf of execution —

how users write successful prompts. We report a candidate phenomenon, an 

integration bottleneck , that lies in Norman’s gulf of evaluation : a repair -relevant 

contribution reaches the user and fails to become actionable at the point of receipt. 

Two cases in an eighteen -case corpus of publicly shared AI -assisted -development 

accounts report this, from a peer and from the system’s own output; both fall at 

evaluation’s interpretation stage, and a third, which would fall at comparison, is 

reached only on an inferential reading and reported as a boundary case. A within -case 

contrast is consistent with actionability turning on whether the contribution can be 

restated as an instruction without an intervening judgement. We report this as a 

candidate warranting dedicated study, not an established regularity; its evidence base 

is retrospective author self -reports. On the submission side, medium alone did not 

sort the corpus contrasts; immediate uptake aligned with a checkable condition across 

four decisive contrasts, while a fifth case shows checkability sufficient for immediate 

uptake did not guarantee persistence. A twenty -trial multi -turn probe across two 

current models observed no constraint loss in fifteen judgeable narrow trials, and a 

ten -trial extension with an explicit restructuring request none in eight. Study 2 is not a 

replication attempt: it isolates regeneration, the mechanism the corpus authors 

invoke, and removes it as a sufficient explanation. We specify four interaction 

requirements for an accepted -constraint ledger; a conformance analysis of twelve 

mechanisms found none documented to satisfy all four. 

Keywords : human -AI interaction; gulf of evaluation; AI -assisted software 

development; prompt repair; external representation; end -user development

# 1. Introduction 

Contemporary AI code assistants place a distinctive burden on their users: to describe 

an intended artifact in natural language, to evaluate the output against an expectation 

that may itself be only partly articulated, and, when it diverges, to revise the 

interaction. That mismatch is often the occasion on which implicit constraints are first 

stated in a form the user can act upon. Three accounts introduce the problem. Peldi 

Guilizzoni (2025), founder of a widely used wireframing tool, reported that six text -

only prompts failed to produce an intended interface and that a subsequently attached 

wireframe produced a satisfactory result within two. A journalist with no coding 

background (Li, 2025) reported that her repair strategy changed only after her 

partner told her she was approaching the task the wrong way. A third user, learning to 

build an application with no prior programming experience (Colban, 2025), rebuilt 

her project three times and identified the reason directly: she could not read the 

generated code well enough to see what had broken. The accounts share a moment of 

divergence and differ in what happened next: the first user attached a representation 

his practice equipped him to produce; the second acquired a strategy from someone 

else’s practice; the third could neither produce a usable representation nor read what 

the system returned. 

Prior HCI research on conversational breakdown has largely examined what the 

system can do to recover (Ashktorab et al., 2019), and work on prompting how to 

write a successful prompt; what users do after a generative system produces 

something they judge inadequate has received less systematic attention in naturally 

occurring settings. Research on multi -turn degradation (Laban et al., 2025) 

establishes that current models lose performance across turns, holding constant by 

construction what the user does when degradation occurs. The present study begins 

where that abstraction ends. 

Research question. When AI -generated output diverges from user intent, what do 

users do to repair the interaction, and under what documented conditions does a 

given repair route become available or unavailable to them? 

Three subordinate questions organize the analysis. SRQ1 : What do users submit after 

an identified divergence, and what property of a submitted artifact is associated with 

the system taking up its constraint? SRQ2 : What configurations arise when repair 

does not resolve monotonically? SRQ3 : Under what conditions does a repair -relevant contribution — from another person, the system, or the user’s own prior work —

become actionable at the point of receipt? 

Contributions. This paper makes three empirical contributions and one design 

contribution. 

First, an account of the receiving side of repair. Two cases document a repair -relevant 

contribution reaching the user and failing to become actionable, from a human peer 

and from the system’s own output. A third, in which the user’s own prior work no 

longer supported recovery of the state needed for comparison, meets our third 

condition only on an inferential reading and is reported as a boundary case 

throughout. We report the phenomenon as a candidate warranting dedicated 

study, not an established regularity. We name it an integration bottleneck and 

locate it within Norman’s gulf of evaluation rather than the gulf of execution (Norman, 

1986; Luger and Sellen, 2016). Both confirmed cases fall at the same stage, 

interpretation ; the boundary case would fall at comparison, so the claim we defend is 

placement within the gulf of evaluation, not distribution across its stages. One case is 

partial in a way that indicates the operative property: of two suggestions from the 

same reviewer, the one that could be restated as an instruction without an intervening 

judgement was executed, and the one that could not was abandoned. 

Second, a definition of what makes a repair artifact effective that can be applied without 

appeal to outcomes, together with preliminary evidence that it is independently 

applicable. The corpus contrasts are not sorted by the visual/textual distinction 

organizing prior work on multimodal prompting; the operative property is whether 

an artifact states a constraint in a form that can be checked. The definition refers to no 

outcome, and uptake and repair success are recorded separately from it, so the 

account is non -circular as stated . We are explicit that this is not the same as having 

demonstrated outcome -independent classification : all coding in this paper, 

including the independent subset audit, was performed on records that narrate what 

the next output did (§4.5). What we establish is that a second analyst applying the 

written definition to the primary sources alone separated the events on the stated 

property; what remains to be established is that the classification survives 

withholding the outcome, and §9.2 identifies that recoding as the verification this 

contribution requires. 

Third, a bounded negative result on constraint persistence. A twenty -trial controlled 

multi -turn probe (Study 2, narrow protocol) established a constraint at turn 1, issued 

an unrelated request at turn 2, and inspected the artifact at turn 3. The constraint 

survived in every judged narrow trial. A ten -trial exploratory extension with an 

explicit restructuring request at turn 2 — single -analyst and single -pass — was 

consistent in its eight judgeable trials. Study 2 does not reproduce the constraint loss 

documented in the corpus; the divergence localizes the candidate explanations to 

conditions the protocol holds constant, without identifying which is at work. Fourth, four interaction requirements for an accepted -constraint ledger , derived from 

documented breakdowns and stated specifically enough to distinguish a conforming 

from a non -conforming implementation, together with a document -level 

conformance analysis (§8.3, not a behavioural test): no shipped persistent -

instruction mechanism surveyed was documented to satisfy them, including the 

automatically generated memory features several now ship, and the research 

prototypes closest to the specification were built for and evaluated on users the 

corpus places outside the problem. We offer the requirements as design hypotheses 

and the conformance analysis as evidence about the documented state of those 

mechanisms, not as an evaluation of the requirements themselves. 

A note on scope. The episodes separate two capacities that accounts of externalizing 

tacit knowledge have often treated as one: articulating a constraint for the system, and 

reading the state the system returns. A user with a professional visual practice could 

not read the code that came back; a user who maintained verbatim prompt archives 

across four tools understood a very small fraction of the generated output. Production 

and reception capacity are dissociable in this corpus, and repair required both (§7.2). 

Every claim is tied to a reconstructed sequence in a documented case or to a recorded 

trial (rules in §4). 

Sections 2 and 3 position the study and state its conceptual commitments; §4 gives the 

method; §§5 and 6 report Studies 1 and 2; §§7 and 8 develop the discussion and 

design requirements; §9 states limitations and concludes. 

# 2. Related work 

2.1 Breakdown and repair in conversational systems 

In conversation analysis, repair refers to the practices through which participants 

address troubles in speaking, hearing, or understanding a preceding turn (Schegloff, 

1992). HCI has adapted this vocabulary to conversational agents, predominantly on 

the system side: Ashktorab et al. (2019) examine user preferences among eight 

strategies a chatbot can deploy after a breakdown, taking the system as the repairing 

party, where the present study inverts it. The integration bottleneck of §5.5 also 

differs from grounding failure in the sense of Clark and Brennan (1991) — grounding 

concerns whether shared understanding is achievable through further interaction, 

whereas these cases concern an already -received contribution that cannot become 

actionable at the point of receipt. 

Luger and Sellen (2016) interviewed fourteen users of conversational agents, found 

expectations substantially out of step with system operation, and read the difficulties 

through Norman’s (1986) gulfs of execution and evaluation. That framing is directly 

applicable here and, we argue in §7, has been unevenly taken up: work on prompting 

addresses the gulf of execution, while the gulf of evaluation has received far less attention in the generative setting — §2.4 discusses emerging exceptions — even 

though generative systems return artifacts substantially larger and less inspectable 

than a conversational agent’s reply. 

2.2 Prompt refinement and query reformulation 

Query -reformulation research documents how users modify queries after 

unsatisfactory results (Huang and Efthimiadis, 2009; Jansen et al., 2005; Qu et al., 

2019). Two differences bear here: reformulation operates on a short query the user 

authored and can re -read, whereas prompt repair operates on an artifact the user did 

not author and may not be able to read; and retrieval returns a ranked list the user 

evaluates directly, whereas generation returns an artifact whose conformance may 

not be observable without executing or reading it. We therefore treat query 

reformulation as a point of contrast rather than a framework to extend. 

2.3 Representations in human -AI interaction 

Recent work has examined non -textual input to generative systems. Wen et al. (2025) 

study how LLMs interpret ambiguous prompts in visualization authoring and 

introduce visual prompts as a complementary modality. Lin et al. (2025) developed 

SketchFlex, in which region sketches guide text -to -image generation. Both establish, 

under designed conditions, that specific visual input can support specific generative 

tasks. Two questions remain open: when users spontaneously introduce non -verbal 

artifacts in naturally occurring repair, and whether the visual/textual distinction is 

the right axis at all. §5.2 reports corpus observations inconsistent with a medium -

based account. Work on artifacts as coordination devices — boundary objects (Star 

and Griesemer, 1989; Blomkvist et al., 2015) — offers partial vocabulary, but the 

analogy has a limit we make explicit in §7: an artifact whose interpretive flexibility 

supports coordination among people may expose no comparable constraint surface to 

a generative system. 

Explainable AI research develops mechanisms by which a system supports the user’s 

determination of what an output means (Miller, 2019; Wang et al., 2019). Two 

differences bear here: XAI typically concerns outputs the user did not author, whereas 

these episodes concern artifacts the system produced from user -authored input; and 

XAI explanations presuppose a reading capacity for the explanation itself, which 

several users in §5.5 lack. 

2.4 Non -experts and code -generating systems 

A body of HCI work examines what happens when non -professional developers work 

with code -generating models. Vaithilingam et al. (2022) studied twenty -four 

programmers using Copilot and reported that the tool did not reliably improve task 

completion time or success rate, and that participants had difficulty understanding, 

debugging, and repairing suggested code. Kazemitabaar et al. (2023) found that access to a code generator did not damage learning outcomes in sixty -nine learners aged ten 

to seventeen; we note this because it constrains a claim in §7.5. 

Liu et al. (2023) is the closest prior work to this paper’s central move. Studying end -

user programmers in spreadsheet data analysis, they identify abstraction matching 

and propose grounded abstraction matching , in which the system translates generated 

code back into a predictable naturalistic utterance so the user can see what it is about 

to do — an intervention on the return path, the direction we argue is under -attended. 

Three differences bear on what we add: their task’s output is directly evaluable by the 

user, whereas the episodes here concern multi -file artifacts not observable without 

reading or executing them; their intervention addresses what the system will do, 

whereas the failures in §5.5 concern what has already been produced; and they 

establish that return -path support helps under designed conditions, whereas we 

document what happens where none exists. 

Ferdowsi et al. (2024) show that continuously displaying runtime values lowers the 

cost of validating AI -generated code. Their intervention and the requirements of 

Section 8 address the same gulf under different conditions: their participants read and 

edit the annotated code, whereas several users in §5.5 report being unable to read the 

returned artifact at all, and their single -file editor contrasts with multi -file projects in 

which the file that changed may not be the file displayed. R1 –R4 relate to Amershi et 

al.’s (2019) guidelines on making system capability clear and providing efficient 

correction, specifying a form of correction persistence not addressed at that guideline 

set’s grain. 

2.5 Multi -turn degradation and constraint regression 

Laban et al. (2025) construct sharded multi -turn versions of six generation tasks and 

report an average performance drop of 39% across the open - and closed -weight 

models tested, decomposing it, over more than 200,000 simulated conversations, into 

a minor loss in aptitude and a larger increase in unreliability. Such protocols hold the 

user constant by construction, foreclosing the question of what users do when 

degradation occurs. Model degradation is the background condition; the distribution 

of repair capacity is the foreground. 

2.6 Distributed cognition and the problem of uptake 

Distributed cognition holds that cognitive work is often accomplished through the 

coordinated activity of multiple agents and artifacts (Hutchins, 1995), and in prompt 

repair the constituent acts — proposing a strategy, submitting, generating a resource, 

producing output, judging conformance, designing the workflow — can be distributed 

across a user, community contributors, and multiple AI systems. This literature 

attends to how contributions are distributed but less to whether a contribution can be 

taken up by its recipient. §5.5 reports cases in which it could not, and §7.2 argues that uptake is a condition on distributed repair the distribution vocabulary does not 

capture. 

# 3. Conceptual and methodological commitments 

The definitions in Sections 3.1 –3.3 were fixed before cross -case comparison and are 

applied uniformly; the coding manual (Supplementary S2) gives the full operational 

rules. 

3.1 Repair -attempt event 

A repair -attempt event is a user submission satisfying four conditions: it follows an 

output the author identifies as diverging from an intended or expected result; it is 

directed at reducing that divergence; it is not the initial input; and a subsequent 

output or author evaluation is recorded. Cases are eligible for event -level analysis 

when at least one such event is reconstructible. A separate proposal -only stratum 

retains cases documenting a concrete repair proposal without evidenced execution; 

those contribute to no event - or configuration -level count and serve only as contrasts. 

Three record types are excluded from event counts: proposal actions (authored by 

someone other than the submitter, counted only if the original poster executes them); 

subsequent non -repair modifications ; and contextual artifacts (persisting across 

stages without being submitted in response to a divergence). 

3.2 Properties of a submitted artifact 

Prior work distinguishes submitted artifacts by medium. The corpus motivates a 

different set of axes, each judgeable from the artifact alone, before any outcome is 

known. We record four: medium (pictorial, textual, or code); provenance (authored 

by the user, extracted from the system’s own output, or preserved from an earlier 

project state); function (diagnostic , addressed to a divergence whose cause is not 

localized; or restorative , addressed to re -establishing a prior state); and checkability 

— whether the artifact states a condition against which a candidate output could be 

judged conformant or non -conformant without further judgement from the user. A 

wireframe encoding column relationships is checkable in this sense; so is a preserved 

working file submitted for comparison; so is a prompt requiring a test alongside a fix. 

A report that something is broken is not. 

Checkability is deliberately separated from system uptake (whether the subsequent 

output reflects the constraint, observed after the fact) and from repair outcome 

(whether the author reports the divergence as resolved). Keeping the three separate 

is what allows the claim in §5.2 to be non -circular by construction : were checkability 

defined by outcome, the finding that checkable artifacts were taken up would be 

tautological. Because the definition is fixed by inspection, the corpus can in principle 

contain a checkable artifact that the next generation did not take up; in the analysed 

corpus no such immediate non -uptake was observed, and §5.2 reports instead a checkable artifact that was taken up in the following generation yet was dropped on a 

later one (Elgali) — a case bearing on persistence rather than on immediate uptake, 

and equally incompatible with a definition that made checkability entail its own 

uptake. The definition and its application should be distinguished. The definition 

is outcome -free; the coding was not performed outcome -blind, since the primary 

sources narrate the next output in the same passage that reports the submission and 

no analyst saw a submission with the response withheld (§4.5). Non -circularity is 

therefore established for the definition and not yet for the judgements made under it. 

Checkability as defined here is binary; a graded version would require a metric we 

cannot supply without appealing to outcomes. It is adjacent to but narrower than 

testability, verifiability, and acceptance criteria (which presuppose an authored 

specification discipline and a party able to run the acceptance procedure): it asks only 

whether the artifact states a condition against which conformance could be judged 

without the user supplying an additional criterion. A preserved working file is 

checkable in this sense while satisfying no definition of a test. 

3.3 Submission and uptake 

Following Norman (1986; see also Norman, 2013 for a later formulation), we 

distinguish two directions. Submission concerns the gulf of execution: whether the 

user can produce something that expresses the intended constraint to the system. 

Uptake concerns the gulf of evaluation: whether the user can determine, from what 

comes back, what state the artifact is now in and what to do next. Uptake failures may 

involve the system’s output or a contribution from a third party; Section 5.5 reports 

both. 

We adopt Norman’s decomposition alongside two alternatives rather than instead of 

them. Conversation -analytic repair (Schegloff, 1992) supplies the vocabulary of 

trouble and its resolution, but §5.5’s cases turn on a stage at which the user cannot yet 

establish that trouble has occurred; distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995) is the 

better vocabulary for §5.4’s restorative artifacts but describes how a system works 

rather than where a user’s action fails. Norman’s decomposition individuates the 

point of failure at the granularity the cases distinguish. 

3.4 Temporal configurations 

Four case -level configurations are defined operationally, each after the first pass 

through the corpus and then applied uniformly (the post hoc definition is a limitation 

stated in §9). Regression : an element the author records as corrected is subsequently 

recorded as reverted, the intervening submission not being a repair for that element. 

Serial collateral breakage : a submission addressing element A produces an 

unrequested change to element B that the author evaluates negatively, on at least two 

occasions. Abandon -and -restart (termed nuke -and -restart in the deposited coding 

materials and audit report): the author abandons the current project state and begins generation afresh rather than continuing targeted correction. Scope violation : the 

system alters material outside the stated target of the request and the author states 

the alteration was not requested. 

3.5 Practice tiers 

Cases are assigned to a tier from the author’s self -description using explicit textual 

criteria. Tier 1 requires a statement of professional practice in software development 

or interface design, or reference to a representational artifact (specification, 

wireframe, version -control diff) held before the episode began. Tier 2 requires 

limited implementation competence together with a recorded practice of systematic 

documentation. Tier 3 requires minimal or absent coding capability at the start. 

Assignment uses only self -descriptions, independently of observed repair behaviour. 

The tier is a presentational index and is used with restraint. It compresses four 

independently varying attributes — domain practice, stated code comprehension, pre -

held representational resources, documented record -keeping — and an author can 

hold any without the others. The subset audit produced exactly this ambiguity (§4.5): 

the Valko disagreement means the boundary is not clean. Two claims rest on the tier 

as a grouping (§§5.6, 7.5); both are descriptive and supported by each author’s self -

description in Table 1, and where an argument turns on a specific attribute those 

sections name the attribute rather than the tier. 

Note on scope of the corpus. Cases are episodes in which the object of the activity is 

producing a working artifact, not acquiring programming concepts; this distinguishes 

the corpus from learning -oriented settings and bounds the applicability of 

Kazemitabaar et al. (2023), as discussed in §7.5. 

3.6 Evidence granularity 

A3 : a verbatim multi -turn sequence is available in the primary source. A2 : individual 

submissions, outputs, or code fragments appear verbatim within an author narrative. 

A1 : state transitions and outcomes are documented in author report without 

verbatim prompts. A0 : stage -level narrative only, underlying interaction inaccessible. 

# 4. Method 

4.1 Design 

We adopted a purposive corpus -based approach to naturally occurring repair 

sequences: researcher -unelicited, publicly shared accounts rather than interviews, 

think -aloud sessions, or controlled tasks. The design captures repair in authentic 

contexts and avoids reactivity, at the cost of the interpretive burden discussed in §4.3. 

Reflexivity. The first author has a professional background in interface design and 

academic training in sociology and cognitive psychology, disclosed as a resource shaping which artifact properties were foregrounded and as a limit. Checkability 

(§3.2) foregrounds what interface -design practice trains its practitioners to notice in a 

submitted artifact, where a linguist might have foregrounded lexical density and a 

software engineer testability; the integration bottleneck (§5.5) attends to the 

receiving side because that practice trains attention to what a user can do with what 

they are given, where a computational modeller might have foregrounded what the 

system produces. These framings are choices, not defects, and readers should treat the 

central claims as informed by them. 

4.2 Corpus construction and composition 

Candidate posts were identified through targeted searches in English and Japanese 

across social, developer, professional, video, and general publishing platforms (Reddit, 

Qiita, Zenn, LinkedIn, YouTube, note.com, Medium, Business Insider, Indie Hackers, 

Stack Overflow Blog). Query families targeted prompt failure and iterative refinement, 

AI -assisted code and interface generation, and community -mediated repair proposals. 

Screening proceeded in rounds. The first applied forty -eight recorded queries against 

a pool of ninety candidates, each assessed against four inclusion conditions: an AI tool 

was used; two or more versions of the submitted text are recoverable; the author 

records a reaction to an output; a revision sequence can be followed. Ten satisfied all 

four; fifty -five documented revision in summary only. Per -candidate breakdown, 

queries, and per -platform pool composition are in Supplementary S1. Subsequent 

rounds extended the corpus toward under -represented practice contexts, principally 

through bylined articles, proceeding from targeted retrieval without a fixed pool, so a 

reader can reconstruct the first round but not those that followed. 

These four are retrieval -screening criteria; the analytic inclusion criterion is narrower 

(§3.1): at least one repair -attempt event must be reconstructible. Eligibility varies by 

level of analysis — cases supporting event -level coding, cases supporting only case -

level configuration judgements, and one proposal -only contrast. 

Relation to the author’s other work. This manuscript is the second in a planned 

three -paper programme on how practitioners externalize implicit constraints when 

working with generative AI. A separately submitted manuscript (Sato, under review at 

the International Journal of Human -Computer Interaction , hereafter Paper 1) examines 

the antecedent moment: how misalignment between AI output and author intent 

triggers the articulation of previously implicit constraints. The two share the framing 

that misalignment occasions externalization but differ in analytic focus and inclusion 

criteria — Paper 1 codes utterances at the moment a constraint is first stated, this 

paper codes repair -attempt events in cases with a reconstructible revision sequence 

(§3.1). 

Relationship of data sources. Paper 1’s analytic set consists of five anonymized 

professional UI/UX designers (P001 –P005) recruited in Japan for a laboratory study conducted between 15 and 25 January 2026, in which each participant produced a 

landing -page hero section under a 30 -minute think -aloud protocol with retrospective 

interviews. The present paper’s analytic set consists of eighteen cases drawn from 

publicly shared retrospective accounts on Reddit, Qiita, Zenn, note.com, LinkedIn, 

Medium, Business Insider, Stack Overflow Blog, Indie Hackers, and GitHub (§4.2), 

identified by their authors’ public names or handles, with primary sources predating 

the January 2026 laboratory window in every case. No participant in Paper 1’s 

laboratory study appears in the present paper’s analytic set, and no case in the 

present paper’s analytic set appears in Paper 1’s laboratory study: the two data 

sources are disjoint by construction, since one consists of researcher -elicited 

laboratory sessions with informed -consent anonymization and the other consists of 

researcher -unelicited public accounts identified by name. The two papers share 

theoretical vocabulary (misalignment as trigger for externalization) but do not share 

cases, utterances, or coded events. No finding reported here is reported as new in 

Paper 1, and no finding reported in Paper 1 is reported as new here. 

The analytic set comprises eighteen cases , partitioned as follows and reported in 

Table 1. 

• Code -generation corpus (n = 13) : cases of AI -assisted code and interface 

generation. All prevalence -style statements in Section 5.3 are reported over 

this corpus. 

• Comparative corpus (n = 5) : cases from general -purpose dialogue and text -

processing tasks, used to examine whether comparable repair structures 

appear outside code generation. These are not tier -assigned and are excluded 

from the denominators in Section 5.3. 

• Repair -event corpus (n = 17) : cases contributing at least one repair -attempt 

event. Case #33 falls in the proposal -only stratum defined in Section 3.1 and 

contributes to no event -level count. 

4.3 Naturally occurring data and its limits 

The materials are researcher -unelicited public accounts, so reactivity toward a 

researcher’s probes is low because no probe exists. They are not unmediated —

subject to retrospective reconstruction, self -presentation, and selection, they should 

be read as author -curated — and where the record includes primary artifacts 

(embedded screenshots, submitted images, full prompt texts) we treat those as more 

directly evidential than the surrounding narrative. Four sources are editorially 

published and the rest self -published; where a claim rests on narrative structure 

rather than a quoted artifact, this heterogeneity is an unresolved source of 

uncertainty. 4.4 Annotation and analysis 

The analysis proceeded in two phases with different evidentiary standing. Phase A 

was structured qualitative content analysis of reconstructed repair events, applying 

the fixed definitions of Sections 3.1 –3.6; the artifact properties (including 

checkability) and temporal configurations were coded here. Phase B was interpretive 

cross -case synthesis, through which the integration -bottleneck reading, the placement 

within Norman’s gulfs, and the design hypotheses of Section 8 were developed. Phase 

A judgements are auditable against the definitions; Phase B judgements are 

interpretive and are marked as such where they appear. 

In Phase A each case was reconstructed into an event sequence recording, per event: 

record status; actor; identified divergence; submitted input; reported subsequent 

output; the four artifact properties of §3.2; system uptake; evidence granularity; 

agency roles. Where the underlying conversation was inaccessible (Case #17), input 

content was not inferred from stage titles or author summaries. In Phase B a cross -

case interpretive pass developed three non -exclusive descriptions of what users did 

— linguistic specification , proceduralization , external representation introduction —

which describe the interaction operation, distinct from §3.2’s artifact properties, 

which describe what was submitted; the same pass identified the temporal 

configurations of §3.4. 

4.5 Analyst configuration and AI assistance 

All primary coding was performed by the first author. A second analyst subsequently 

conducted an independent subset audit. A large language model was used at two 

points and its output was treated as provisional until verified against the primary 

source: candidate retrieval during screening, and a preliminary field -level annotation 

checked line by line. No coding decision reported here rests on an unverified model 

output. 

Independent manuscript -blind subset audit. Six cases, stratified by evidence 

granularity, were recoded by a second analyst with research experience in HCI and 

qualitative methods, no co -authorship relation to the author, and no prior sight of this 

manuscript. The analyst’s completed coding sheet is deposited with the 

supplementary materials. A positionality statement for the second analyst —

disciplinary background, prior familiarity with generative AI tooling, and their own 

stated framing of AI -assisted development — is deposited alongside it. The analyst 

was given the operational definitions of Section 3, the coding manual (Supplementary 

S2), and the primary sources, with no access to the Findings, Discussion, or any prior 

judgement. Coded fields were case inclusion, practice tier, evidence granularity, the 

four temporal configurations, repair -attempt event boundaries, and — for each event 

so identified — the four artifact properties of Section 3.2, checkability among them. We report raw agreement rather than a chance -corrected coefficient. At n = 6 for 

binary judgements, Cohen’s κ (and comparable alternatives such as Gwet’s AC1) is 

dominated by the marginal distribution and uninformative. A coefficient over the 

thirty -two event -level checkability judgements would in principle be computable, but 

the first analyst’s per -event records for two of the four audited cases are narrative 

rather than matched -event tables, so the required paired judgements do not exist; for 

the two cases where matched pairs do exist (Case #12: 1 and 1; Guilizzoni: 7 by the 

first analyst and 6 by the second, within a ±1 boundary tolerance) all paired 

judgements agreed. We report agreement per field, name every disagreement, and 

treat the absence of a coefficient as a limitation (§9.1). Supplementary S5.5 gives the 

underlying calculations. 

Agreement was four of six on case inclusion, five of six on practice tier, three of six on 

evidence granularity , and twenty of twenty -four (83.3%) on temporal 

configurations. We report every coded field rather than the favourable subset. 

Evidence granularity is the weakest on exact agreement, and we give the informative 

decomposition: all three disagreements (Guilizzoni A2/A3, Case #12 A1/A2, 

Narasimhan A1/A0) are single -step displacements on an ordinal scale, so agreement 

is three of six exact and six of six within ±1 level . We read this as the scale’s cut -

points being underspecified in the coding manual rather than as unreliable case 

reading, and record it as such. Every disagreement and its resolution is itemized in 

Supplementary S5.5. The second analyst identified thirty -two repair -attempt events 

across the four cases from which they judged events recoverable and assigned 

checkability to each: twenty -seven checkable, five not. The five turn on a single 

property — each reports that a defect persists without stating the condition under 

which it would be resolved. 

The audit shows that an analyst who had not read this manuscript could apply the 

written distinction to complete source records and separated the events on the 

property Section 5.2 identifies. Because the primary sources narrate what the next 

output did, the analyst saw outcomes while coding; the audit does not establish 

outcome -independent classification. An outcome -blinded recoding is identified in §9.1 

as the most useful single addition to this evidence base. 

4.6 Source corroboration 

One case admits a check unavailable elsewhere. Zimin’s narrative is accompanied by a 

public repository containing the prompt log for the documented work, so narrative 

claims can be compared against a record produced contemporaneously and for a 

different purpose. This check and the subset audit (§4.5) are independent: the audit 

tests whether a second analyst working from the primary source alone recovers §3.2’s 

coding, whereas this tests whether a narrative agrees with a prompt log by the same 

author. We call it within -case source corroboration rather than triangulation for that 

reason: agreement reduces the concern of retrospective misremembering for logged events but not that of self -presentation. §5.1 reports both a claim the log supports and 

one it constrains. A weaker corroboration is available in Valko’s account, where build 

records maintained for an external programme requirement accompany the narrative.

All materials are publicly accessible on the platforms named in Section 4.2. No private

Ethics approval and institutional determination. The study analyses researcher -

unelicited, publicly shared texts authored by individuals writing about their own 

experience with commercially available AI tools. No intervention, interaction, or 

communication with any author occurred as part of data collection or analysis, and no 

identifiable private information was obtained beyond what each author elected to

courtesy notification concerning naming, described under Author -contact record

Institutional determination. Formal ethics committee approval was not sought, and 

we state the reason rather than leaving it to inference. The study involves no 

recruitment, no intervention, no communication with any author as part of the 

analysis, and no data not already published by its author under a public byline.

Where an author is characterized in terms of capability limits, the characterization is

# 5. Findings 

5.1 What users submitted 

Three non -exclusive operations describe what users did after divergence. Linguistic 

specification covers repairs staying within language: rephrasing, elaborating, adding 

negative constraints, adopting domain vocabulary. In several Tier 1 cases successive 

submissions made a description more discriminating through domain -specific 

vocabulary — Case #10 escalated from broad evaluative wording to design terms for 

colour, typography, spacing, and layout. Among Tier 3 cases it takes a distinct form: 

Sajor’s repair attempts remained confined to resubmitting error messages verbatim, 

the tool’s explanation of its own fix being unusable to a non -coder. She reports a 

graphic design background used in adjusting the interface, yet repair of functional 

defects remained lexically bound — a visual practice did not transfer to a domain 

whose relevant constraints were not visually inspectable. Proceduralization covers repairs adding explicit instructions about how the 

interaction should proceed. Community members in the comparative Reddit case 

authored a structured procedural formulation the original poster incorporated and 

executed; Case #46 restructured a long input into a declared multi -part sequence; 

Case #75 introduced an approval gate. Elgali maintained verbatim prompt records 

across four tools and introduced a capitalized instruction as a constraint -

reinforcement device after observing an established constraint dropped. Narasimhan 

routed a specification through a general -purpose dialogue system before submitting it 

to the code -generation platform, assigning distinct functions to distinct systems. 

External representation introduction covers artifacts — wireframes, images, 

structured documents, preserved file versions, error outputs — alongside or instead 

of linguistic reformulation. 

5.1.1 Evidence from a complete prompt log 

Zimin’s public prompt log records 195 numbered entries across four labelled sections 

and permits two checks. Consider one defect: a list selection that disappeared when 

the user interacted with an adjacent panel. Nine consecutive submissions address it —

seven brief negative reports, one specific description of the failure condition, and a 

final submission reporting resolution. The specific description occurs three turns 

before resolution, and the submission immediately preceding resolution carries no 

new information. The sequence does not support attributing resolution to the 

informative prompt, and we do not attribute it. What it shows is a ratio: across 

nine turns addressing one defect, one submission carried new semantic content and 

eight did not. A parallel eleven -turn sequence in the same log shows the same pattern 

under stronger conditions: nine submissions are near -verbatim null reports and new 

information enters twice (details in Supplementary S5). We describe this as a state in 

which the user has little remaining vocabulary with which to differentiate one attempt 

from the previous one; it occurs in the record of the corpus’s most technically 

resourced participant, and its frequency cannot be estimated where no verbatim log 

exists. 

5.2 What made a submitted artifact effective 

The corpus contains four submissions that a medium -based account would sort 

incorrectly. Guilizzoni’s wireframe and Case #8’s game -board image are both 

pictorial; the first was followed by an output the author reported as satisfactory, the 

second by an outcome reported as unresolved (the system reporting that it could not 

reliably localize board positions). Valko’s reframing prompt and Sajor’s resubmitted 

error string are both textual; the first was followed by targeted resolution of a defect 

that had resisted repeated correction, the second by a sequence the author reports as 

unproductive. On the properties of Section 3.2, the sorting is different. Guilizzoni’s wireframe is 

checkable: it states column relationships and navigation placement as conditions a 

candidate output either meets or does not. Case #8’s board image is not: it depicts a 

state without stating a condition, and recovering the condition requires an inference 

the system reported it could not perform reliably. Valko’s prompt is checkable by an 

explicit device: it requires that a test confirming the fix be produced alongside the fix. 

Sajor’s error string reports that something failed without stating what a corrected 

state would satisfy. 

Two further artifacts occupy the checkable position by routes a medium -based 

account has no vocabulary for. Zimin submitted a hand -preserved working version of 

a file alongside the broken one and asked the system to compare and repair the 

difference: what is submitted is the intended state itself rather than a description of it, 

and the user’s contribution is the preservation and the framing. Valko, when 

corrections failed to reach an intended element, used the browser’s developer tools to 

locate it and submitted the copied code marked as the repair target: the artifact was 

extracted from the system’s own output, and what the user supplied was the selection. 

One case constrains the claim, and we state precisely what it constrains rather than 

leaving it to be inferred. The association we report is between checkability and uptake 

within the generation immediately following submission ; on that association all 

four decisive contrasts above are consistent, and each of the two alternative routes to 

checkability was likewise taken up in the next generation. Elgali’s episode constrains a 

different claim — not immediate uptake but persistence across an episode. His 

capitalized restatement of the integer -currency constraint is checkable — it states a 

condition on stored values — and was reflected in the generation that followed, yet 

the author records it being dropped again on a later regeneration. The evidential 

base for the immediate -uptake association is therefore the four decisive 

contrasts and the two alternative routes to checkability, in a purposive corpus 

of eighteen; Elgali’s case is the counter -example that bounds the association’s 

scope, showing that checkability sufficient for immediate uptake was not 

sufficient for persistence. The association we report holds within the generation 

immediately following submission, not across an episode. 

In the decisive contrasts above, submissions stating a checkable condition were 

followed by outputs reflecting that condition, and those reporting a state without 

stating a condition were not, irrespective of medium. Supplementary S5 tabulates 

checkability against immediate uptake for every principal submission in the corpus, 

and we state plainly that we do not report that table as an aggregate finding : its 

rows are organized by principal submission described in the primary record , a unit not 

constant across cases, and within -case dependence is not controlled. We deposit it so 

that a reader can see the counts the qualitative claim was formed against, not as 

evidence in its own right. At event level, a source independent of the first author bears on the distinction’s 

applicability. In the manuscript -blind subset audit (§4.5), a second analyst 

reconstructed thirty -two repair -attempt events across four cases and coded twenty -

seven checkable and five not; all five non -checkable events fall in one case and each 

reports a defect persisting without stating the condition under which it would be 

resolved. That result bears on independent applicability of the distinction from the 

written definition; it does not establish outcome -independent classification and does 

not quantify the association with uptake. The immediate -uptake association rests on 

the four missorted submissions, the two alternative routes to checkability, and the 

within -case contrast in §5.5 — with Elgali’s episode bounding its scope to the 

immediately following generation — not on a rate.

5.4 Diagnostic and restorative purposes 

Valko’s reframing prompt and Elgali’s prompt archive are both checkable textual 

artifacts serving different work. The prompt was diagnostic : applied to a state whose 

cause was not localized, requiring holistic analysis and a test establishing whether the 

defect persisted. The archive was restorative : a record of the constraint set that had 

produced a prior stable state. Narasimhan illustrates the axis by absence — prompt 

archaeology names the situation in which a restorative artifact is needed and 

unavailable. Elgali refines it: facing the same problem he retained two restorative 

artifacts and deliberately excluded a third (the requirements document, on the 

grounds that it recorded intentions rather than results). The two functions are 

orthogonal, and an artifact optimized for one can obstruct the other. Colban’s mid -

episode adoption of version control is the corpus’s only instance of a restorative 

resource acquired by a Tier 3 author within an episode. 5.5 When a contribution arrived and could not be used 

§5.1 concerns what users submit; a distinct phenomenon concerns what a user can 

receive and put to work. We call a state in which a repair -relevant contribution 

reaches the person and cannot be converted into an action an integration 

bottleneck , reported as a candidate phenomenon rather than an established 

regularity. 

A case is counted only where the record establishes three things: a specific repair -

relevant contribution was available; the author did not act on it; and the author states 

a reason concerning their own capacity to convert it, rather than a decision that acting 

was unnecessary. Condition (3) does the work — an author who judged a suggestion 

not worth applying is not an instance, whereas one who reports being unable to 

determine what it would require is. No published retrospective account can 

independently settle could not act from did not act ; we treat every instance at the 

strength of a self -report and identify in §9.2 the observational design that would 

replace the inference with an observation. 

Two cases meet all three conditions on the author’s explicit statement; a third meets 

the third condition only on an inferential reading and is set out separately as a 

boundary case. 

From a human peer. Sajor received code reviews from developer friends. One 

suggestion — adding descriptive class names — was relayed to the tool and applied 

successfully; of a comment about inlined styling, the author reports being unable to 

determine whether the issue lay in the repository upload or the code itself. The 

within -case contrast is consistent with actionability turning on whether a suggestion 

could be relayed without an intervening judgement the author could not supply : “Use 

descriptive class names” is executable as stated, whereas “the styling is inlined” 

requires the recipient to determine where and why before it becomes an instruction. 

From the system. Colban received generated code across successive requests and 

reports that its unreadability prevented both defect identification and the formulation 

of a corrective instruction: the contribution was present and complete, and what was 

missing was the capacity to extract an action from it. 

Boundary case: from the user’s own prior work. Narasimhan sought to recover a 

prior stable state and found the available artifacts did not support recovery. Condition 

(3) is met only on a reading under which the inability concerned extracting the prior 

state from those artifacts rather than their absence, and the primary record does not 

settle which; the second analyst judged the case inadmissible for event -level coding 

(§4.5). Case #17 is indeterminate: its author designed a workflow selecting and 

resubmitting AI -generated critiques, but the record does not establish the selection 

rule. The comparative corpus supplies the contrasting case. In the Reddit comparative case, 

community members authored a structured procedural formulation and the original 

poster incorporated elements of it into a self -executed repair reported as successful: a 

contribution that arrived from a peer and did become actionable. What distinguishes 

it from Sajor’s un -relayable suggestion is the property §7.2 identifies — the 

community formulation was already stated as a procedure the recipient could execute, 

requiring no intervening judgement about where or why to apply it. We note the 

contrast rather than treat it as a controlled comparison: the two cases differ in task 

domain as well as in the property at issue. 

The cases share the failure and differ in what could not be done with what arrived. 

Norman decomposes evaluation into perceiving, interpreting, and comparing. 

Colban’s failure is at interpretation: the artifact was present and complete, its 

meaning not recoverable. Sajor’s is also at interpretation, under an externally supplied 

description. Both confirmed cases therefore fall at the same stage. Narasimhan’s, 

on the boundary reading only, would fall at comparison, and we do not treat the 

corpus as establishing a comparison -stage instance. What the confirmed cases 

support is placement of the phenomenon within the gulf of evaluation; distribution 

across its stages is a possibility the boundary case raises and the corpus does not 

settle. We make no claim to an exhaustive typology. The evidence base is two 

confirmed cases and one boundary case, all at A1 granularity, each established from 

the author’s own account. The source of the un -integrable contribution differs across 

the cases — a peer, the system’s own output, and the user’s own prior work — so the 

phenomenon is not tied to a class of contributor. 

5.6 Practice -linked variation 

Cases differ in the resources available before the episode began (§3.5, Table 1). Tier 1 

authors introduced practice -furnished representational repertoires — wireframes, 

Markdown specifications, version -control diffs — without constructing them; Elgali 

(Tier 2) compensated for limited implementation competence through disciplined 

record -keeping; five Tier 3 authors began without established representational 

resources for the domain. Zimin bounds the account from the well -resourced end: 

professional capability across submission and uptake, and nevertheless a state in 

which changes to one file caused others to be regenerated. Professional capability 

raises the ceiling without removing the underlying condition. 

Tier 3 outcomes are set out per case in Supplementary S6. Two features carry the 

argument. Sajor and Narasimhan constructed no retained resource within the episode 

and report, respectively, an application whose code the author judges poor and cannot 

improve, and prototypes that functioned while repair beyond that stage reached a 

limit. Colban and Valko each constructed one — Colban restorative (version control 

adopted mid -episode), Valko diagnostic (a reframing prompt requiring a test, plus 

developer -tools extraction) — reporting a working application on the third rebuild and a publicly released application. Li’s route was neither: the revised approach came 

from outside, and the outcome is a working page at reduced scope. All outcome 

characterizations are the authors’ own. 

Note on Valko’s inclusion in Tier 3. The second analyst’s audit placed Valko at the 

opposite end of the tier scale (§3.5), reading demonstrated capability within the 

episode rather than self -described starting capability. We retain the assignment made 

under the definition (self -description at start), so the disagreement affects how the 

tier is read rather than the trajectory, which is documented from the primary source 

and is not in dispute. 

The five began on comparable stated resources, and tier does not determine outcome 

within Tier 3. Two of the five constructed a resource within the episode and the two 

are of different kinds — Valko’s diagnostic, Colban’s restorative. Within Tier 1, Case 

#17 exhibited serial collateral breakage despite practitioner -level resources. Practice 

resources are associated with the range of routes available; the corpus does not 

establish that they determine which route is taken. 

5.7 An observation about the corpus itself 

Of the eighteen cases, one provides a complete prompt log (A3), eight reproduce 

prompts or code fragments (A2), eight document repair through stage -level summary 

(A1), one a high -level account only (A0). In the first screening round ten of ninety 

candidates satisfied all four inclusion conditions while fifty -five — the modal outcome 

— documented that revision had occurred while summarizing away the sequence. 

Producing a reconstructible record of one’s own repair sequence is itself a 

representational practice, and within the corpus it tracks §3.5’s tiers: the fullest 

record from the professional developer, partial records from the semi -expert and the 

non -expert who kept a build journal for an external requirement, none from the rest. 

A corpus of published accounts therefore over -represents users holding the capacities 

this paper argues are unevenly held, and the fifty -five summarized accounts are the 

visible shadow of the population it under -represents. 

# 6. Study 2: Constraint persistence across generations 

6.1 Purpose and design 

§5.3 documents cases in which a constraint the user had established was absent from 

later output. The corpus cannot separate two candidate mechanisms: whether the 

constraint was never learned by the model in the affected turn, or whether it was 

carried into that turn and subsequently displaced. Study 2 addresses the second, using 

a four -turn protocol on two current commercial models. 

What Study 2 is and is not. It is a mechanism probe testing one hypothesis — that an 

intervening unrelated request displaces an established constraint — not a comparative evaluation and not a benchmark; the models are treated as two instances 

of a class of system. 

Each trial ran four turns in a fresh chat session. T0 asked for an initial shopping -cart 

module with no monetary -representation constraint. T1 instructed the model to store 

and compute all monetary values as integer minor units, float and Decimal 

prohibited in the money path. T2 asked for a method returning the three most 

expensive items by descending unit price — logically independent of the T1 

constraint. T3 asked for the current full module contents. Prompts were constant 

across trials (Supplementary S4). For each trial we recorded whether the T1 output 

satisfied the constraint ( T1 gate ) and whether the T3 output still did ( T3 

judgement ); a trial failing the gate was excluded from the persistence denominator. 

Judgements were made by inspection, distinguishing docstring or comment mentions 

of float /Decimal from their operative use in money -path expressions. 

6.2 Materials and execution 

Ten trials per model were conducted on Claude Sonnet 5 (Anthropic) and ChatGPT 

5.5 (OpenAI), a total of twenty, all recorded as executed on 20 July 2026 

(UTC+09:00) through each provider’s web interface. These two systems were 

selected because they are the commercial models most frequently identified by name 

in the corpus source accounts (§§5.1 –5.6) and represent two of the three major public 

frontier -LLM families as of July 2026; the probe is about these two instances of a class 

of system, not the class. Because those interfaces do not expose model snapshot 

identifiers or decoding parameters, snapshot identifiers, temperature, top -p, and 

maximum -token settings are unspecified rather than reconstructed retrospectively. 

The model labels reported are those displayed at execution. 

Module size and scope. The T0 module produced by each model was a single -class 

Python module of approximately 40 –100 lines, containing between four and eight 

methods and no external dependencies beyond the Python standard library. The 

narrow protocol T2 request (adding a top -priced -items method) required a single 

method addition; the broad extension T2 request (rename the class, reorder methods 

into state -modifying and query groups, rewrite the top -level docstring) required 

restructuring within the same module. This small -module scope is referenced as a 

limitation in Section 7.3: results do not generalize without further study to multi -file 

projects, modules with external dependencies, or modules substantially larger than 

the Study 2 materials. 

Session order and conditions. The sheets fix execution order only to the resolution 

of the trial identifiers, assigned in execution order within each model: T -01 to T -10 

(Claude Sonnet 5) preceded T -11 to T -20 (ChatGPT 5.5), and B -01 to B -05 preceded B -

06 to B -10 on the same systems. Model was therefore blocked rather than 

interleaved and is confounded with position in the execution sequence ; the design cannot separate them, and a replication should randomize or counterbalance. 

Time of day, inter -trial interval, and browser -session reuse were not recorded, so 

within -block order effects, session -state carryover, and fatigue cannot be analysed. 

The narrow sheets record 20 July 2026 for all twenty trials; the broad -extension 

sheet carries no date entry , so any statement that the broad trials shared that date 

rests on the author’s working practice rather than the record. Each trial ran in a fresh 

chat session with no shared history at the model level (§6.1); interface -level carryover 

cannot be excluded (§6.5). 

The protocol — the four prompts, the T1 gate criterion, and the T3 judgement rule —

was fixed in writing before the first trial was executed. The record -integrity exclusion 

rule described below was the one rule developed after execution, and its derivation 

and uniform application are documented. The study was not preregistered in a 

public registry. The full T0/T1/T2/T3 transcript for every trial, together with the 

per -trial judgement and its supporting rationale, is provided in Supplementary S4. 

Broad extension materials. The broad extension (Section 6.4) comprises ten further 

trials, five per model, executed through the same interfaces in the same preparation 

cycle (date not recorded on the sheet; see Session order and conditions above), with 

identifiers B -01 to B -10. Its protocol differs in one turn: T2 was replaced by an explicit 

refactoring request — rename the class, reorder methods into state -modifying and 

query groups, rewrite the top -level docstring, and output the complete module. T0, 

T1, and T3 are unchanged and the same gate and judgement rules apply. These trials 

received no second -pass confirmation and no subset audit, and were judged by 

a single analyst on a single pass. 

Second -pass confirmation and record -integrity exclusions. After the initial per -

trial judgement was recorded for the narrow protocol, a second pass through the 

twenty transcripts checked whether each trial’s four -turn record supported the 

judgement. The pass was not blind — the first pass’s judgements were visible — so we 

call it a confirmation pass and report a confirmation rate rather than an inter -rater 

coefficient. Eighteen trials were confirmed and two (T -03, T -05) excluded as record -

integrity defects, leaving eighteen intact four -turn records of which seventeen passed 

the T1 gate; both exclusions are documented in Supplementary S4. The pass was 

conducted by the first author, and the direction of its removals — two trials the first 

pass had recorded as preserved , not as lost — reduces the numerator of preserved 

trials, which is conservative against a paper claiming preservation. Supplementary S5 

gives the T1/T2/T3 cell contents for T -03 and T -05 and the features that identify 

them as record -integrity defects rather than mis -recorded violated trials.

6.4 What the results support, and what they do not 

Direct conclusion. A constraint established at T1 was carried through an unrelated 

request at T2 and remained visible at T3 in every one of the fifteen judged narrow 

trials with intact records. Under this protocol, on this constraint type, at this scale, 

Study 2 does not reproduce the constraint loss documented in §5.3. 

Exploratory extension: explicitly broad requests. A single -analyst, single -pass 

extension (ten additional trials, five per model, executed in the same preparation 

cycle; the sheet carries no date entry, §6.2) replaced the T2 prompt with an explicit 

refactoring request. Eight of ten trials passed the T1 gate (B -03 on Claude and B -08 on 

ChatGPT failed). In all eight the constraint was preserved at T3, and the T3 output was 

a complete module in which the money path used integer minor units throughout. In

instructed re -emission is not evidenced for that trial. Preservation and manipulation 

verification are separate counts: preservation was 8/8; instructed re -emission was 

verified for 7/8. Within -chat whole -module re -emission is not sufficient, on its own, to 

reproduce the loss the corpus documents. Whether agentic regeneration that overwrites files across a multi -file project behaves the same way is a distinct question 

these trials do not address.

What isolating within -chat re -emission establishes. Study 2 does not reproduce 

the corpus setting, and the four differences below make that explicit; its value does 

not depend on reproducing it. The corpus authors explain their own losses by 

regeneration — Case #12’s author attributes the reverted type to the system 

regenerating the file, and Elgali the dropped currency constraint in the same terms —

and regeneration is the one condition of the four a within -chat protocol can isolate, 

since whole -module re -emission is its observable counterpart. Study 2 holds turn 

distance, artifact scale, and salience at values favouring retention, applies 

regeneration, and observes no loss. The inference is therefore about the corpus 

authors’ account rather than the corpus setting: regeneration alone, under 

conditions maximally favourable to detecting its effect, did not produce the loss 

they attribute to it. That removes one candidate from an explanation offered in the 

primary sources — a more modest thing than a failed replication. 

An account the results do not support. The natural reconciliation — that corpus 

regressions followed requests broad enough to force regeneration, whereas Study 2’s 

T2 was narrow enough to be satisfied additively — is not supported. Seven of 

seventeen gate -passing narrow trials produced whole -module re -emissions with the 

constraint surviving in all seven; all eight gate -passing broad trials received an explicit 

restructuring instruction and preserved the constraint, with re -emission evidenced in 

seven. The bounded conclusion is: within -chat whole -module re -emission alone was not 

sufficient, in the observed trials, to reproduce the loss the corpus documents. The probe 

did not test multi -file agentic regeneration, file writes the user does not see, or long -

horizon project state. Whole -module re -emission is treated as the operational 

counterpart of what corpus authors describe as regeneration; the correspondence is 

behavioural and the equation is ours. 

Remaining candidate differences between probe and corpus. Since within -chat 

re -emission alone did not reproduce the loss, the remaining candidates lie in what the protocol holds constant. Four are visible and Study 2 cannot discriminate among 

them. Turn distance : T1 and T3 are two turns apart, against corpus constraints 

established tens or hundreds of turns before the loss. Artifact scale : one module in one 

file, against multi -file projects. Constraint salience : T1 is a turn whose entire content is 

the constraint. Visibility : T3 places the whole artifact before the user as text, whereas 

the corpus tools vary on this axis in ways not consistently recoverable. Of these, 

visibility is the one the paper’s account predicts should matter (§7.2); we flag it as a 

candidate the data motivate rather than establish, and §9.2 sets out the comparison 

that would test it directly. 

The T -12 self -correction. ChatGPT 5.5’s T -12 trial responded to the T1 instruction 

incorrectly and failed the T1 gate, using Decimal rather than integer minor units, then 

at T2 spontaneously converted the module to integer cents while adding the 

requested method. We draw no conclusion from a single trial, but flag the pattern. 

6.5 Limitations of Study 2 

Ten trials per model supports no inferential test. Treating each protocol’s trials as 

independent Bernoulli draws — a strong assumption, since trials within a model 

share a prompt, an occasion, and an unrecorded snapshot, so the true bound is wider 

than the nominal one — a Clopper –Pearson 95% one -sided upper limit on the 

underlying loss rate is 0.181 for the fifteen judgeable narrow trials and 0.312 for the 

eight judgeable broad trials. We do not pool them: the protocols differ in T2 and in 

judgement configuration. The broad extension’s 0.312 bound is wide enough that its 

eight trials license no population -scale claim on their own, and it is retained as a 

corpus -consistent supplementary observation rather than an independent 

confirmation of the narrow result. 

Model was blocked rather than interleaved in the execution order (§6.2), so any drift 

in interface state or analyst judgement across the sequence is confounded with model; 

the study reports no between -model comparison and none should be read from it. 

Access was through consumer web interfaces without recorded snapshot identifiers 

or decoding parameters. The T2 prompt was held constant, so the response -form 

contrast in Table 5 is observed rather than assigned; it supports the negative 

conclusion but not a positive claim that response form is irrelevant. Two trials were 

excluded as record -integrity defects and the confirmation pass was not blind. The 

broad extension is single -analyst and single -pass: gate failures reduce its denominator 

to eight, instructed re -emission removes response -form variation, and B -01’s T2 

response does not evidence instructed re -emission, so the manipulation is verified for 

seven. The study addresses one constraint type in one task domain over four turns. 

Most consequentially, the protocol differs from the corpus settings in at least four 

respects it cannot separate — turn distance, artifact scale, constraint salience, and 

visibility — so its failure to reproduce the corpus losses locates the explanation 

among these without identifying which. 7. Discussion 

7.1 Why medium alone is insufficient 

A natural reading of the multimodal prompting literature is that supplementing 

language with images improves outcomes. §5.2 reports four submissions inconsistent 

with that reading and proposes checkability as the property that sorts them. 

The proposal is not that pictures do not help but that they help when, and because, 

they state a condition. Guilizzoni’s wireframe helps because column relationships and 

navigation placement are conditions a candidate interface either satisfies or does not, 

where the same author’s prose did not carry those conditions in a form the system 

acted on; Case #8’s board image fails for a reason internal to the same account, since a 

depiction of a state is not a statement of a condition and recovering one requires an 

inference the system reported it could not reliably make. The medium finding in prior 

work is a special case: sketches help in visualization authoring and region -based 

image generation because there spatial relations are the constraints. Where the 

relevant constraints are not spatial — a data type, an approval step, the absence of a 

regression — a spatial artifact carries nothing. 

This is where the boundary -object analogy reaches its limit. Boundary objects 

coordinate across communities partly because they admit multiple readings (Star and 

Griesemer, 1989; Blomkvist et al., 2015); checkability requires the opposite. Star 

(2010) subsequently warned against invoking the concept as a synonym for any 

shared artifact; the artifacts in Section 5.2 are not boundary objects in her stricter 

sense, and we invoke the concept only to mark the axis on which they differ. 

7.2 The gulf of evaluation 

The paper’s central theoretical claim is a relocation. Work on prompting, prompt 

engineering, and multimodal input addresses the gulf of execution: how a user 

expresses an intention in a form the system will act on. The difficulties in §5.5 lie on 

the other side: Sajor could not convert a peer’s observation into an instruction; Colban 

could not extract an action from the system’s own output; Narasimhan could not 

determine, from available artifacts, what the project had contained when it last 

worked. In each case the material needed for repair was present; what was missing 

was the capacity to determine, from what came back, what state the artifact was in. 

Luger and Sellen (2016) read conversational -agent difficulties through both gulfs; the 

generative setting sharpens the asymmetry. The gulf of execution has been widely 

addressed — prompt libraries, templates, sketch input, the operations of §5.1 — and 

the gulf of evaluation far less, which is where the corpus suggests user differences 

concentrate. The two are not levels of one skill: Elgali maintained disciplined prompt 

archives while understanding a very small fraction of the generated code. The integration bottleneck is our name for a candidate failure specifically at the gulf of 

evaluation. Both confirmed cases in §5.5 fall at interpretation; the boundary case 

would fall at comparison but is not treated as establishing that stage. The relocation 

we defend is therefore from the gulf of execution to the gulf of evaluation, not a 

mapping onto the latter’s internal stages. Sajor’s partial case indicates the mechanism 

most precisely: what made one peer suggestion usable and another not was, on the 

reading the contrast supports, whether it could be restated as an instruction without 

an intervening judgement. This is a property of the interface between the contribution 

and the user , not of the contribution or the user alone. 

Two consequences follow for design (§8). Detection is separable from comprehension 

— Elgali detected a dropped currency constraint despite minimal code 

comprehension, because fractional values are observable in behaviour, whereas 

Colban’s undetected defects were structural — and the relevant intervention is on the 

return path. 

A further consequence concerns how this work is usually described. Putting a 

requirement into a form the system can act on has been treated as a single 

accomplishment; the episodes here do not divide that way. Sajor specified interface 

changes through a professional visual practice and could not read the code that came 

back; Elgali maintained verbatim prompt archives while comprehending a very small 

fraction of the generated output; Valko constructed a checkable diagnostic prompt 

from linguistic resources alone and reached the element he needed to fix only through 

the browser’s developer tools. Repair with a generative system therefore requires two 

analytically distinct capacities running in opposite directions — articulating a 

constraint for the system, and evaluating the state the system returns — and the 

corpus contains cases in which the first was constructed within an episode (§7.5) but 

none in which the second was. This dissociation is what makes “ externalization of 

tacit knowledge” insufficient as a description of a single accomplishment covering both 

directions . The claim is not that the construct fails — externalizing an implicit 

constraint is precisely what the production side names — but that it decomposes: 

production and reception of externalized constraints are separable capacities, and the 

corpus places its heaviest demand on the second. Nothing in the argument requires 

distinguishing pre -held from newly formed constraints. 

7.3 The maintenance burden and where it becomes invisible 

§5.3 documents constraints established and subsequently lost; §6 put the same 

structure to two models and did not reproduce it. Study 2 is not a replication attempt: 

it is a mechanism probe testing whether within -chat re -emission alone suffices, and its 

null result narrows the candidate mechanisms without contradicting the corpus 

documentation. The obvious reconciliation — that corpus losses follow from 

regeneration and the protocol avoided it — is unavailable, because the protocol did 

not avoid it (Table 5). Of the four candidate differences in §6.4, the visibility candidate has corpus counterparts worth stating: Colban’s unrequested authentication library 

went into persistent configuration unnoticed, Zimin’s tool removed licence and 

documentation files during unrelated work, and Elgali detected a dropped constraint 

only because fractional values are visible in behaviour. A constraint dropped and 

displayed will be recorded by an author looking at it; one dropped and not displayed 

can be recorded only once its consequences surface. The calibration stated in §6.4 

applies unchanged. 

The maintenance burden §8 addresses does not depend on settling this. Whether 

constraints fail more often in agentic tools or fail at similar rates but are noticed less, 

the user’s position is the same: re -establishment draws on both gulfs, and a user 

unequipped on the evaluation side may not detect that repair is needed. 

7.4 Alternative explanations 

Four alternatives bear on the corpus, and one concerns the corpus itself. 

A general expertise gradient. More experienced developers repair better, and the 

two gulfs are symptoms of one competence. Two observations resist it: Elgali is high 

on execution -side capacity and low on evaluation -side, and Sajor holds a professional 

visual practice yet cannot read returned code. A single -gradient account treats these 

as measurement noise; a two -gulf account predicts them. 

Tool or task, rather than user. Current tools may break down as project state grows, 

for everyone. Zimin’s case is the strongest evidence and we accept it in part; what it 

does not explain is variation in what happened next — Zimin diagnosed the 

mechanism and stopped by decision, where Colban could not determine what had 

broken. Tool immaturity may set the condition under which repair becomes necessary 

without determining what is available once it does. On a variant, the operative 

variable is domain experience rather than a repertoire of representations; this is close 

to our own framing, which is why the phrasing is practice -linked rather than skill -

based. Sajor (a transferable practice that did not transfer) and Valko (no domain 

experience, checkable artifact constructed from linguistic resources alone) are where 

the two diverge. 

Change in the systems, rather than in the phenomenon. The corpus was 

assembled from accounts posted predominantly in 2025 and Study 2 executed in mid -

2026 (§9.1). Post -training work across that interval may have targeted multi -turn 

constraint retention, in which case Study 2’s preservation reflects a capability the 

corpus’s earlier models did not consistently hold. This is compatible with a stable 

phenomenon becoming less visible and with a genuinely reduced one; the design 

cannot distinguish them, and providers publish no comparable snapshot identifiers 

for the consumer -web interfaces used here. The corpus as a performance. Writing a reconstructible public account itself 

requires a substantial part of the evaluation -side capacity this paper argues is 

unevenly held (§5.7). The corpus therefore under -represents the severe end of the 

phenomenon, and the integration bottlenecks observed may be those an author could 

reconstruct in retrospect rather than those experienced. Whether a retrospective 

account indexes the moment it describes is why §9.2 identifies a study conducted 

while repair is under way. 

7.5 An implicit evaluation demand 

§§5.6 and 7.2 describe a distribution of repair capacity. We resist the generic framing 

— that AI tools disadvantage novices — because it is unfalsifiable at that level of 

abstraction. The corpus supports a narrower claim: in the analysed episodes, 

continued repair placed a demand on the evaluation side that the low -entry 

framing under which these users adopted the tools did not make explicit. 

The claim is stated over an attribute, not over the tier. §3.5 commits to naming the 

operative attribute wherever an argument turns on one; here it is stated code 

comprehension at the start of the episode , a single self -described property rather than 

the four -attribute composite the tier compresses. Five authors state minimal or absent 

code -reading capability (Colban, Li, Narasimhan, Sajor, Valko) and one comprehension 

of a small fraction of generated output (Elgali); the claim concerns these six on that 

attribute alone and would be unchanged if the tier scheme were withdrawn. This 

matters because the subset audit disagreed about Valko’s tier (§4.5) while not 

disputing the self -description the argument uses. 

The claim has three parts. First, several authors approached the tools as low -entry 

routes to building software — Colban states this directly, and Narasimhan, Sajor, Li, 

and Valko frame their episodes comparably. Second, the episodes reveal a capability 

the premise does not mention: determining, from what the system returns, what state 

the artifact is in. Sajor published an application whose code she judges poor and 

cannot improve; Colban rebuilt three times because she could not locate what had 

broken; Narasimhan reached a state from which no prior configuration could be 

recovered. Third, acquisition of the missing capability within an episode was not 

observed. Was not acquired and was not recorded as acquired are distinguishable, and 

episodes end where the author stopped writing; across eighteen accounts spanning 

days to months none narrates the acquisition, and acquiring the capability requires 

reading the very output the user cannot read. Valko constructed execution -side 

vocabulary mid -episode from linguistic resources; Colban’s three cycles show the 

evaluation -side barrier persisting as other competences accumulated. 

Stated this way the claim is falsifiable and design -actionable: a corpus of episodes in 

which authors who state minimal code -reading capability routinely acquire it within 

an episode, or in which repair proceeds without it, would refute it. If the demand falls on the evaluation side, the remedy is not training users to prompt better — the input 

side is the one they can already work — but supplying the evaluation capability in the 

interface (§8). Kazemitabaar et al. (2023) found no worse learning outcomes for 

learners given a code generator, in a differing setting (scaffolded authoring tasks, a 

designed environment, three weeks, with concept acquisition as the object); our claim 

concerns whether evaluation -side capability is acquired within a build episode as a by -

product of repair pressure , not whether it can be taught. 

A vocabulary caution. What varies across the corpus is not aptitude but the 

distribution of representational resources — a prior artifact, a documentation habit, a 

colleague who reads code — produced by professional trajectory and by which tools 

are marketed to whom. The asymmetry documented here is a property of an 

interaction in which one party’s output is offered as intelligible and is not. 

# 8. Design requirements 

The relocation argued in §7.2 makes these the requirements to state. If the binding 

constraint were on the submission side, the remedy would be to help users express 

constraints better and the mechanisms surveyed in §8.3 would already be the right 

shape. The corpus locates it on the return path instead, and the requirements follow: 

entry on confirmation (R1) because what fails is a state the user had verified , not 

merely stated; the paired check (R2) because a constraint that cannot be checked 

cannot be reported as violated; observable -terms notification (R3) because the user 

who most needs the report cannot read the implementation; and provenance (R4) 

because a record the user cannot audit reproduces the opacity the ledger exists to 

remove. 

In each direction below the corpus contains a user who built the mechanism by hand, 

and that user was among the better -resourced; treating each as interface 

infrastructure relocates a cost currently borne by those least equipped for it. All are 

design hypotheses ; none has been implemented or evaluated. They address the 

persistence -and -detection subset of the receiving -side problem, not the whole of it —

neither Sajor’s un -relayable peer suggestion nor Colban’s unreadable artifact would 

be resolved by a ledger alone. A research prototype built for professionally 

experienced users has independently reached parts of the same specification (§8.3), 

reported as convergence rather than validation. 

8.1 An accepted -constraint ledger 

Case #12 and Elgali’s episode document a confirmed correction undone by a later 

generation. A record of user -confirmed constraints, exposed to subsequent 

generations and visible to the user, addresses the re -establishment burden of §7.3. 

Study 2 shows a stated constraint can be carried across an intervening generation, so 

the ledger’s warrant does not rest on models being generally incapable of retention; it rests on the corpus cases where retention nonetheless failed and, more importantly, 

on the user having had no way to be told. 

R1 — Entry on user confirmation, not on statement. A constraint enters the ledger 

when the user confirms that an output satisfies it, not when the user first states it. 

Study 2 shows that stated constraints are honoured within a generation and can 

survive the next; Case #12 and Elgali’s episode show that confirmed ones are 

nonetheless sometimes not carried, and that the user may learn this late or not at all. 

The ledger’s function is persistence and notification, not specification. 

R2 — Expression in a checkable form. Each entry pairs the constraint with the 

check that established it: for Elgali’s currency constraint, an assertion over stored 

values; for Valko’s defect, the test his prompt requested. Entries admitting no check 

are recorded as unverified and marked as such. 

R3 — Notification on violation, in observable terms. When a later generation 

violates an entry, the system reports the violation in terms of behaviour the user can 

observe rather than the code producing it. This generalizes the condition under which 

Elgali detected a dropped constraint despite minimal code comprehension, and is the 

requirement that acts on the gulf of evaluation directly. 

R4 — User -editable, with provenance. Entries can be retired by the user, and each 

carries the turn at which it was established, so that a ledger accumulated over a long 

session remains inspectable rather than becoming an opaque constraint set. 

Figure 1 sketches one arrangement satisfying the four. Whether an interface satisfying 

R1 –R4 reduces the burden it addresses, and whether it does so differentially across 

users who differ in stated code comprehension, is an empirical question this study 

does not answer. Figure 1: An interaction sketch for the accepted -constraint ledger.

8.2 Three further directions 

Approval before change to persistent state. Case #75’s author built an approval gate by 

hand. The scope -violation cases document the system altering persistent project 

material — replacing a dialog, deleting documentation files, adding an unrequested 

authentication library — with no confirmation step; making that confirmation a 

default rather than a device a sophisticated user must know to author addresses what 

Colban’s case documents. 

Surfacing constraints in observable form. R3 generalizes beyond the ledger: rendering 

a constraint as an observable property of output extends detection to users who 

cannot audit an implementation, a lever on the gulf of evaluation that does not require 

raising technical competence. Feasibility varies by constraint type — fractional 

currency is observable, an absent index is not. Structured failure reporting and reconstruction support. Valko’s diagnostic prompt 

took seven prior failed attempts to arrive at; an interface eliciting the same structure 

(what was expected, what occurred, how many attempts failed, what test would 

confirm a fix) would supply what he assembled by hand, and a practitioner post 

proposes a comparable structure deliberately (Zhang, 2025). Separately, prompt 

archaeology names the condition in which a prior working state cannot be 

reconstructed; retaining provenance of generated states and making prior states 

restorable addresses it, as Colban’s mid -episode adoption of version control did by 

hand. These two rest on thinner evidential support than R1 –R4. 

8.3 Conformance analysis: do current mechanisms satisfy R1 –R4? 

This subsection is a document -level analysis, not a behavioural test. Twelve 

mechanisms are assessed: ten shipped persistence, scoping, or memory mechanisms 

identified in vendor documentation, and two research prototypes targeting rule 

persistence. Judgements were made against R1 –R4 from vendor documentation and 

accompanying preprints on the access dates recorded in the reference list. Each cell of 

Table 7 records ✓, partial , or —; absence of documentation is not treated as evidence 

of absence in the tool. Judgements were not independently double -coded, and a 

behavioural audit is identified in §9.2 as a future step. 

Current AI coding tools ship persistence mechanisms in three classes. Class 1 — user -

authored statement persistence : a file of instructions loaded into context at session 

start (Claude Code’s CLAUDE.md , Copilot’s .github/copilot -instructions.md ,

Cursor’s project rules, Windsurf’s rules files, the AGENTS.md convention). Class 2 —

scoped or hierarchical instruction : a refinement of Class 1 binding an instruction to a 

path or file pattern (Copilot’s glob -scoped instructions, Cursor’s per -rule activation 

modes, Windsurf’s workspace -versus -global scopes, Claude Code’s directory -walking 

lookup); what is added is control over where an instruction applies. Class 3 —

automatically generated memory : notes the system writes for itself from the 

interaction, without the user authoring a file (Claude Code’s auto memory, Cursor’s 

Memories, Windsurf’s Cascade memories); entry is triggered by the system’s salience 

judgement — often a user correction — and the store persists across sessions. 

The differences matter for R1 and R4. Class 3 comes closest to entry that is not merely 

user statement, and is still not what R1 specifies: R1 requires entry on the user’s 

confirmation that a given output satisfies the constraint , whereas Class 3 entry follows 

the system’s salience judgement and records no verification of an output. Class 3 also 

weakens R4, since an automatically written store is less directly editable than a user -

authored file. All three classes are therefore R1 partial in Table 7 for different reasons, 

and the table does not resolve them into a finer grade the documentation does not 

itself distinguish. Against R2 and R3 the classes converge, and the convergence is the finding. No 

surveyed mechanism pairs an entry with the check that established it (R2); 

instruction files and memory stores alike are prose. None monitors subsequent 

generations against its own entries or reports a violation in observable terms (R3) —

Claude Code’s memory documentation states explicitly that both of its mechanisms 

are treated as context rather than enforced configuration — so a rule a later 

generation silently contradicts produces no notification in any surveyed tool. 

The gap has a precise shape. Every shipping mechanism addresses the gulf of 

execution; Class 2 additionally saves the user from restating instructions in the wrong 

place. None addresses the gulf of evaluation. 

Concurrent research systems. Two research prototypes, both preprints at the time 

of writing, reach parts of the specification. Ma et al. (2026) introduce ZORO, which 

anchors rules from an existing rules file to each step of an agent’s plan and requires 

the agent to submit code evidence — and, for rules marked testable , an executable 

unit test — before advancing; a controlled evaluation reports higher rule adherence 

with the system than without. Sharma (2026) compiles agent instruction files into 

executable guardrails against context drift . An empirical evaluation of repository -level 

context files reports that supplying such a file tended to reduce task success across 

multiple agents and models (Gloaguen et al., 2026); statement persistence without 

violation reporting predicts a null result, not a negative one. 

Against the requirements, ZORO satisfies R2 and R4, does not satisfy R1 (its ruleset is 

imported from a user -authored file), and satisfies R3 in part (violations surface as 

unproven rules and failing tests — implementation terms, not observable behaviour). 

Both prototypes share a population premise: each presupposes a user who maintains 

a rules file and can read implementation -level evidence, capacities the corpus records 

users lacking. The pair of requirements this corpus is distinctively positioned to add is 

therefore R1 and R3.

# 9. Limitations and conclusion 

9.1 Limitations 

Single analyst and audit scope. One person developed the framework, selected the 

cases, verified the AI -assisted preliminary annotation, defined the configurations, 

assigned tiers, and interpreted the results (the second analyst’s role is confined to the 

subset audit). An independent manuscript -blind subset audit (Supplementary S5.5) on 

six cases agreed on case inclusion in four of six, on practice tier in five of six, on 

evidence granularity in three of six, and on temporal configurations in twenty of 

twenty -four judgements (83.3%). We did not revise Table 1 or Table 2 in 

consequence, but this should not be read as the disagreements being immaterial to 

them: the three evidence -granularity disagreements and the Valko tier disagreement 

bear directly on columns of Table 1, and the four temporal -configuration disagreements on cells of Table 2. We retain the first analyst’s assignments because 

they follow the written definitions (tier from self -description at episode start; 

granularity from what the primary source reproduces verbatim), and we record the 

alternative readings rather than absorbing them. Three further boundaries remain: 

the audit was not outcome -blinded; it did not extend to uptake or to the integration -

bottleneck judgements of §5.5; and per -event agreement was computable only for 

Case #12 (1 and 1) and Guilizzoni (7 and 6, within ±1 tolerance), the other two 

audited cases having narrative rather than matched -event records. The outcome -

blinded recoding identified in §9.2 is the verification on which the paper’s second 

contribution depends (§3.2). 

The checkability –uptake association is not quantified. Section 5.2 reports the 

association qualitatively; the S5 annotation table is organized by principal submission 

described in the primary record, and that unit varies across cases. A quantified 

association would require a canonical event -level table with uptake recorded 

separately from later persistence and within -case dependence handled explicitly —

constructible only for the A2 and A3 cases. 

Post hoc definitions. The four temporal configurations were defined after a first pass 

through the corpus. They were then applied uniformly. 

Selection and sample size. The corpus is purposive, comprises eighteen cases, and 

supports no prevalence claim. Every quantified statement is an existence claim, a 

within -corpus fraction scoped as such, or a Study 2 trial count; central claims survive 

at n = 1 per phenomenon because they concern what the interaction can produce, not 

how often. Six of the seven cases added after the first eleven exhibit a temporal 

configuration; we cannot exclude that non -monotonic content influenced their 

selection. The selection procedure for those seven was not blind to the presence of a 

temporal configuration in candidate accounts, and the differential inclusion rate was 

not recorded contemporaneously; this bounds what selection can be ruled out for the 

temporal -configuration counts, and future work would record it prospectively. Section 

5.7 states a second selection effect: publishing a reconstructible account is itself a 

representational capacity, so the corpus over -represents users who hold the 

capacities the paper argues are unevenly distributed. 

Screening reproducibility and source heterogeneity. The first screening round is 

reconstructible; the rounds adding the remaining cases proceeded from targeted 

retrieval without a fixed pool, so a reader can audit what they retained without 

reproducing the search that found it. Four sources are editorially published and the 

remainder self -published, and we did not distinguish author -original from editor -

imposed narrative structure. Cases span ten months and multiple tool versions, so 

tool -version differences are confounded with case differences. Temporal separation between corpus and probe. The corpus was assembled from 

public accounts posted predominantly in 2025; the narrow Study 2 trials are recorded 

as executed on 20 July 2026, and the broad extension carries no recorded date (§6.2). 

Providers revise consumer -web models without public snapshot identifiers, so a trial 

on the same -labelled model on a later date is not guaranteed to reproduce these 

judgements. The corpus -probe divergence in §6.4 is therefore confounded with time; 

a change in the phenomenon cannot be separated from a change in the systems, and 

the four candidate differences listed in §6.4 do not exhaust the possibilities on that 

account. 

Evidence granularity. Nine of eighteen cases are documented at A1 or A0, so uptake 

is judged from the author’s report of what the next output did rather than from 

inspection. Case #17’s underlying conversation was inaccessible. 

Study 2. Section 6.5 states the study -specific limitations; the most consequential is 

that the protocol differs from the corpus settings in at least four respects it cannot 

separate. 

Language and platform. The corpus is confined to English - and Japanese -language 

platforms, so any practice specific to other language communities is outside its range. 

The integration bottleneck rests on two confirmed cases and one boundary case ,

all at A1 granularity, each established from the author’s account of being unable to act 

rather than from an independent record. The boundary case (Narasimhan) satisfies 

the third condition only on an inferential reading, and the second analyst judged it 

inadmissible for event -level coding. We report the phenomenon accordingly 

(Abstract; Section 5.5). 

9.2 Next steps 

Three studies follow from the divergence in §6.4. The first manipulates what Study 2 

could only observe: the four -turn protocol with T2 assigned rather than fixed — one 

arm requesting a single -method addition, the other a restructuring — distinguishing 

the effect of being asked to restructure from that of a model electing to re -emit. The 

second manipulates visibility: the same constraint and intervening request in a chat 

interface returning the artifact as text each turn versus an agentic file -editing 

configuration writing to disk and showing a summary; if constraint loss is comparable 

across conditions and detection is not, that separates a property of the model from a 

property of the interface. The third addresses the receiving side directly: think -aloud 

or screen -recorded sessions in which a contribution arrives and the researcher 

observes whether it is taken up, replacing an inference from narrative with an 

observation. That design also states the condition under which the candidate would 

be withdrawn: if observed sessions show contributions of the kind §5.5 describes 

being converted into action at rates comparable to those the same users achieve on self -authored artifacts, the phenomenon reported here is better explained as an 

artifact of retrospective reporting than as a state arising during repair. 

Three smaller studies would strengthen this evidence base without new data 

collection: a focused audit of the integration -bottleneck criterion, with an analyst 

judging each of §5.5’s three conditions separately; an outcome -blinded recoding of 

checkability by a third analyst receiving the submitted artifact alone with the 

response withheld; and a behavioural audit of R1 –R4 against the twelve mechanisms 

in Table 7, running each against a scripted violation scenario. An evaluation of R1 –R4 

against the practice tiers remains outstanding, since the requirements have not been 

implemented. 

9.3 Conclusion 

The principal answer concerns the receiving side. Two cases document a repair -

relevant contribution arriving and failing to become actionable — from a peer and 

from the system — with a third, in which the user’s own past work no longer 

supported the required comparison, reported as a boundary case. Both confirmed 

cases fall at the interpretation stage of evaluation. Integrability was consistent with 

whether the contribution could be restated as an instruction without an intervening 

judgement the user could not supply. We report the phenomenon as a candidate 

warranting dedicated study; it sits at the gulf of evaluation, comparatively less 

addressed than the gulf of execution. A second answer concerns what users submit: 

across the four decisive contrasts, immediate uptake aligned with whether an artifact 

stated a checkable condition rather than with its medium, while one further case 

shows that checkability sufficient for immediate uptake did not guarantee persistence 

across an episode. A third describes when repair did not resolve monotonically: the 

four temporal configurations of §3.4 provide a vocabulary, and within Tier 3 the 

operative property was whether a restorative resource was available or could be 

constructed within the episode (§§5.4, 5.6). 

A twenty -trial narrow probe and a ten -trial single -analyst extension across two 

models produced no observed constraint loss in the fifteen judgeable narrow or eight 

judgeable extension trials, narrowing the candidate explanations to conditions 

differing between protocol and corpus — among them how much of the artifact the 

user is shown, which we flag as a candidate rather than establish. 

A user who cannot determine what state the artifact is in does not merely repair more 

slowly; that user may not detect that repair is needed. Interfaces recording what a 

user has confirmed and reporting violations in observable terms would relocate the 

burden — and no shipped persistence mechanism surveyed, including the generated 

memory features now appearing across these tools, meets the specification under 

which that could be tested. Declaration of generative AI and AI -assisted technologies in the writing
